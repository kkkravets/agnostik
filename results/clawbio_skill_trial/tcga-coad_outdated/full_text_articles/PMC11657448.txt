# Plasma mutation profile of precursor lesions and colorectal cancer using the Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay

Authors: Mariana Bisarro dos Reis, Wellington dos Santos, Ana Carolina de Carvalho, Adhara Brandão Lima, Monise Tadin Reis, Florinda Santos, Rui Manuel Reis, Denise Peixoto Guimarães

Journal: BMC Cancer

PMCID: PMC11657448

PMID: 39695441

DOI: 10.1186/s12885-024-13287-2

Canonical source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657448/

## Abstract

## Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide. Early detection of precursor lesions or early-stage cancer could hamper cancer development or improve survival rates. Liquid biopsy, which detects tumor biomarkers, such as mutations, in blood, is a promising avenue for cancer screening.

## Aim

To assess the presence of genetic variants in plasma cell-free tumor DNA from patients with precursor lesions and colorectal cancer using the commercial Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay.

## Material and methods

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) samples from the plasma of 52 Brazilian patients were analyzed. Eight patients did not have any significant lesions (five normal colonoscopies and three hyperplastic polyps), 24 exhibited precursor lesions (13 nonadvanced adenomas, 10 advanced adenomas, and one sessile serrated lesion), and 20 patients with cancer (CRC). The mutation profile of 14 CRC-associated genes were determined by next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay in the Ion Torrent PGM/S5 sequencer.

## Results

Thirty-three variants were detected in eight genes (TP53, PIK3CA, FBXW7, APC, BRAF, GNAS, KRAS, and SMAD4). No variants were detected in the AKT1, CTNNB1, EGFR, ERBB2, MAP2K1 and NRAS genes. All variants were considered pathogenic and classified as missense or truncating. The TP53 gene harbored the most variants (48.48%), followed by the KRAS gene (15.15%) and the APC gene (9.09%). It was possible to detect the presence of at least one pathogenic variant in cfDNA in 60% of CRC patients (12/20) and 25% of precursor lesions (6/24), which included variants in three patients with nonadvanced adenoma (3/13 – 23.08%) and three with advanced adenomas (3/10 – 30%). No variants were detected in the eight patients with normal findings during colonoscopy. The detection of mutations showed a sensitivity of 60% and a specificity of 100% for detecting CRC and a sensitivity of 50% and a specificity of 100% for detecting advanced lesions.

## Conclusion

The detection of plasma NGS-identified mutations could assist in early screening and diagnostic of CRC in a noninvasive manner.

## Full article

## Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer death for both men and women worldwide, accounting for more than 9% of all cancer deaths [1]. The estimated incidence for Brazil, according to the Brazilian National Cancer Institute, is over 45,000 CRC cases for 2023, presenting an elevated cancer mortality rate [2, 3]. In this scenario, the early diagnosis enables a timely treatment at the initial stage of the disease and is recognized as an effective way to improve the survival rate of patients [4, 5].

Currently, colonoscopy and noninvasive methods, such as the fecal occult blood test, have been used in CRC screening as strategies for reducing mortality caused by this cancer [5, 6].

Although colonoscopy-based screening has proven to be highly effective in detecting precursor lesions and CRC, it is a costly and invasive procedure with a risk for complications; therefore, the large-scale use of this approach is challenging [7]. Stool-based tests also have limitations, associated with false-positive results leading to unnecessary endoscopic examinations (mainly guaiac-based tests) and a false-negative rate of approximately 35% [8]. Moreover, the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) sensitivity for advanced adenomas can be as low as 40% [9].

In addition, a simple, non-invasive test with high sensitivity for detecting colorectal cancer and advanced precancerous lesions could enhance participation and adherence rates, ultimately leading to reduce the incidence and mortality of this cancer.

Our group recently published the initial findings from a colorectal cancer screening initiative in a specific region of Brazil. The program utilizes the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) as the initial screening method, followed by a diagnostic colonoscopy for positive cases. Our results highlighted impressive metrics, including high test completion rates, overall participation, successful colonoscopy procedures, and early cancer detection at its initial stages. Nevertheless, we did observe a notable occurrence of positive FIT results not aligning with positive findings during subsequent colonoscopies [10]. Therefore, new molecular screening strategies are emerging to improve early CRC detection [11, 12] and screening outcome results [13].

The analysis of circulating cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) in blood is a promising noninvasive approach for CRC detection and management [14, 15]. Recently, knowledge of the molecular profile of lesions has led to the development of new, noninvasive tests based on detecting these molecular alterations. Over the last few years, the rapid development of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has made it feasible for clinical application. Recently, a multi-test was developed (CancerSeek) for detecting multiple types of cancer, including CRC, combining the detection of mutations in ctDNA using NGS and determining the levels of circulating proteins [16].

Our group recently reported the mutational profile of precursor lesions and CRC in lesions of a Brazilian population [17, 18]. Herein, we intend to evaluate the mutation profile in liquid biopsy (plasma) from patients with precursor lesions, and colorectal cancer samples from the Barretos Cancer Hospital CRC screening program using the NGS Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay, which contains 14 colorectal cancer-related genes.

## Material and methods

### Study population and sample collection

We evaluated 52 patients who underwent colonoscopy for diagnosis or after a positive FIT test at Barretos Cancer Hospital from 2015 to 2019 [10, 19]. The Research Institutional Board of the Barretos Cancer Hospital approved the study (753/2013 and 1060/2015), and all patients signed informed consent before enrollment.

The detected lesions were classified according to the WHO criteria [20] as previously reported [10]. Subjects with a personal history of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) or Lynch syndrome and a prior history of CRC or other types of cancer were excluded. Tumors were staged according to the Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th edition TNM staging system [21]. When more than one lesion was detected per patient, the classification was based on the worst lesion detected following the order of severity: adenocarcinoma (cancer), advanced adenoma (AA), nonadvanced adenoma (NAA), sessile serrated lesion (SSL), and hyperplastic polyp (HP).

Patient peripheral blood was collected in EDTA tubes (BD vacuntainer®), and after centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the plasma was separated, stored in aliquots, and frozen at -80 °C until processing [22].

### Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) isolation and quality control

After a second centrifugation step of 16000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, cell-free DNA was isolated from 1 to 4 mL of plasma by using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's recommendations. This kit is specifically designed to extract nucleic acids from liquid samples, ensuring efficient recovery of cfDNA.

The isolated cell-free DNA was quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), which allows for specific and precise quantification of small amounts of DNA.

To assess the purity and size distribution of the cfDNA, we utilized the TapeStation 2200 (Agilent®) with a High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape system (Agilent®). This system provides a visual representation of DNA quality, enabling us to confirm that the cfDNA meets the necessary criteria for downstream applications.

### Mutation profile by next-generation sequencing

The cfDNA was used for variant detection of 14 CRC-associated genes (AKT1, APC, BRAF, CTNNB1, EGFR, ERBB2, FBXW7, GNAS, KRAS, MAP2K1, NRAS, PIK3CA, SMAD4, and TP53) using the commercial Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austin, TX). In brief, 1 to 27 ng of cell-free DNA was used to amplify 49 amplicons covering 236 hotspots mutation and insertions/deletions in 14 key colorectal cancer-related genes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were quantified using the Ion Library TaqMan® Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific®). The quantified libraries were pooled and subjected to automated preparation and enrichment using 50 pM of purified libraries in Ion Chef System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and Ion 540 Kit-Chef (Thermo Fisher Scientific®). Sequencing was performed using Ion PGM Hi-Q View Sequencing Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and Ion S5 Sequencing Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific®) on Ion Torrent PGM/S5 sequencer, respectively.

The resulting sequencing data was processed in the Torrent Server, and the Human genome build 19 (hg19/GRCh37) was used as the reference genome for aligning the sequenced reads using the Torrent Mapping Alignment Program (TMAP) in Torrent Suite™ Software (Thermo Fisher). Variant calling and annotation were performed using the workflow for Oncomine Colon Liquid Biopsy—w1.8 – DNA in the Ion Reporter™ Software (version 5.18), a specific workflow designed for liquid biopsies with default parameters.

### Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (v.21) and R software (v. 3.6.1). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test and the chi-square test. The nonparametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc correction. Sensitivity, or positive percent agreement (PPA), is defined as (True Positives)/ (True Positives + False Negatives) and indicates the fraction of patients with specified lesions positive for mutation. Specificity, or negative percent agreement (NPA) is defined as (True Negatives)/(True Negatives + False Positives), and indicates the proportion of normal samples that are correctly expected to be negative for the mutation. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

### cfDNA and NGS quality control and metrics

Due to variations in cfDNA concentrations among samples obtained during the isolation step and a specified volume limit for input during library preparation, the total mass of cfDNA utilized fluctuated. Among the 52 cases, a median of 17.94 ng (range of 6.64 to 27.06 ng) of cfDNA was used as input for sequencing analysis, with 14.9ng (range 8.94- 20ng) for normal samples, 20ng (range 18.98-20ng) for hyperplastic polyps, 16.77ng (range 7.09-20ng) for non-advanced adenomas, 19.16ng (range 10.27–20 ng) for advanced adenomas, 12.3ng for the only SSA sample analyzed, and 13.30ng (range 6.64 -27.07ng) for cancer.

The median value of all samples for median read coverage was 9658 (range 2021–28617), and the median molecular coverage was 1354 (range 320–3221). The distribution of median read coverage and the median molecular coverage according to the type of lesion, the input of cfDNA, and the chip used for sequencing are represented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The average number of reads per functional molecule with a unique molecular index was 7.96 (± 6.6). The average coverage per amplicon, number of mapped reads, on target percent and mean depth per sample are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1.

### Genetic variants detected in ctDNA of precursor lesions and colorectal cancer

We observed genetic pathogenic variants in 34.62% of the participants (18 of 52). No detectable variants were identified in participants with normal colonoscopy or hyperplastic polyps or in SSL participants. It was possible to detect the presence of at least one variant in the ctDNA of 6 adenoma participants (6/24 – 25%), including variants in 3 patients with nonadvanced adenoma (3/13 – 23.08%), 3 with advanced adenomas (3/10 – 30%) and twelve with CRC (12/20 – 60%).

The presence and number of plasma mutations per case were greater in cancer patients than in the other participants, and they were significantly greater in CRC patients when compared with participants with normal colonoscopy or hyperplastic polyps (p = 0.044 and 0.031, respectively) (Table 2 and Fig. 1A). ## Discussion

In the present study, the feasibility of a noninvasive mutation-based biomarker was tested using plasma samples and NGS in Brazilian CRC screening program participants and colorectal cancer patients.

A total of 33 mutations were identified in 52 samples, with a greater percentage of mutated samples (60%) and an average number of mutations (1.27 mutations per sample) in CRC samples, compared to precursor lesions (adenoma) (25% of samples analyzed harbored mutations and 0.27 mutations per sample). Notably, using the same sequencing panel, Eikenboom et al. [23] observed a mutation rate of 0.6 mutations per sample in CRC, and mutations were not detected in AA. Low or no mutation rates were detected in colorectal precursor lesions using other sensitive techniques, such as QClamp XNA-based PCR and droplet digital PCR [24, 25]. Although cfDNA is a stable molecule with potential for CRC screening analysis, the proportion of ctDNA and the amount released by tumors in precursor stages and early stages of cancer can be a challenge in the analysis of mutations in the plasma of screening candidates [26]. This fact is reflected in the low number of mutations detected in precursor lesions in this study, even in advanced adenomas. In addition, the allele frequency of detected mutations was greater in cancer samples, corroborating the data showing that more advanced lesions release more DNA molecules into the bloodstream.

Studies have demonstrated the potential for the detection of precursor lesions using markers based on gene expression [27, 28] or DNA methylation [29], in addition to DNA mutation [25, 30]. For instance, specific methylation changes in tumor suppressor genes have been identified as early indicators of malignancy, providing valuable insights for biomarker combinations together with mutation analysis. Enthusiastic results were recently reported by simultaneous analysis of cfDNA genomic alterations, aberrant methylation status, and fragmentomic patterns using the Shield ™ test [31]. The authors showed a sensitivity of 83.1% for CRC and 13.2% for advanced adenomas and sessile serrated lesions, with a specificity of 89.6% for the test [31]. Our group previously reported that the combination of SEPT9 and BMP3 methylation analysis in ctDNA from plasma samples and age > 60 years achieved 74.0% sensitivity and 54.0% specificity for detecting Advanced Adenoma + CRC compared to normal samples [32], indicating that despite good sensitivity, cfDNA methylation presented poorer specificity when compared to mutation biomarker in the current work. Moreover, incorporating protein biomarkers can further refine the diagnostic process. Of note, the cancerSEEK test, a multi-analyte test that simultaneously evaluates the presence of mutations and eight cancer-associated protein biomarkers in blood, presents high accuracy for CRC detection [16].

Herein, we obtained a ROC curve (AUC 0.8) value, indicating the good performance of ctDNA mutation in terms of sensitivity and specificity in detecting CRC. However, for adenoma detection, the sensitivity decreases to 30%, which, in part, can be justified by the low concentration of ctDNA released from precursor lesions. Similar results were observed using a sequencing cfDNA panel, with a low sensitivity (53.8%) and high specificity (92.3%) [33].

Limitations in the use of plasma molecular assays for screening of asymptomatic, average-risk individuals and early detection rely on the suboptimal limit of detection for early stage CRC and precursor lesions. The release of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and its detection are associated with initial tumor burden, suggesting that tumor volume and the presence of metastasis are significantly related to the detection of ctDNA in plasma [34, 35]. In addition, the small proportion of ctDNA in the total circulating cell-free DNA (the fraction of ctDNA that can span less than 0.01%) constitutes another issue to overcome in detection methodology and the feasible implementation for clinical application [36, 37]. Another justification for the low sensitivity of adenoma and early-stage cancer detection relies on the performance of the Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay. The recommended input of 20 ng of cfDNA is essential for reliably detecting rare variants, especially those at a frequency of 0.1% (one variant in a background of 999 wild-type DNA templates). However, achieving this requirement consistently can be challenging due to variability in cfDNA recovery from plasma. Furthermore, adenomas tend to be small size, and do not exhibit the prolonged apoptosis or necrosis seen in advanced cancers [25], which may account for the discrepancy in detection rates between cancer and precursor lesions.

Despite the low sensitivity for detecting nonadvanced lesions, in our study, no mutated ctDNA was detected in participants with normal colons. This high specificity demonstrated for the mutation-based biomarker could render this test a good candidate to complement stool-based tests in clinical practice and thus avoid unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures. Due to the small number of samples analyzed, stratification by cancer stage for mutation analysis was not performed.

In conclusion, our study showed that detecting gene mutations through minimally invasive plasma sampling can aid in identifying individuals with colorectal cancer and those with precursor lesions. The choice between using a commercial Oncomine Colon cfDNA Assay or a more sensitive assay remains debatable. While our findings demonstrate the assay's feasibility, it is crucial to acknowledge the need for further research to validate these results clinically. The following steps involve exploring enhancements to the assay's sensitivity and specificity, particularly for identifying precursor lesions. Implementing this assay in routine screening programs could prioritize identifying individuals with cancer and significantly improve early detection of CRC. Still, thorough clinical validation will be essential to ensure its effectiveness and reliability in diverse patient populations.
