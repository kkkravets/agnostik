# Real-World Genomic Landscape of Gastrointestinal Cancers in Asia and the Middle East Using Comprehensive Circulating Tumor DNA Next-Generation Sequencing

Authors: Manabu Muto, Yu Sunakawa, Nippun Sandhir, Yi Hsin Liang, Shaheenah Dawood, Nitesh Rohatgi, Ankur Bahl, Steve Olsen

Journal: Oncology Research and Treatment

PMCID: PMC12316443

PMID: 40324346

DOI: 10.1159/000545560

Canonical source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12316443/

## Abstract

## Introduction

Gastrointestinal malignancies account for 25% of all cancer cases and 35% of cancer-related mortality. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) can elucidate the genomic landscape of gastrointestinal cancers; tissue-based genotyping has traditionally been used, but liquid biopsy-based genotyping is a noninvasive alternative. Moreover, geographical variations in the genomic landscape of gastrointestinal cancers have not been fully elucidated. This retrospective study aimed to gain insight into the genomic landscape of patients with gastrointestinal cancers from the Asia and Middle East (AME) region using plasma-derived circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA).

## Methods

From routine clinical practice, 2,601 plasma samples were collected from 2,062 patients with gastrointestinal cancers in the AME region. NGS profiling was conducted using the Guardant360® assay. The frequency of biomarkers that can aid decision-making in cancer patients was investigated.

## Results

Single-nucleotide variants affected most commonly TP53 (70.4%), KRAS (44.0%), APC (25.7%), ATM (15.1%), and PIK3CA (12.3%). Copy number alterations were most often observed in EGFR (13.7%), CCNE1 (5.9%), PIK3CA (5.0%), MYC (4.7%), and FGFR1 (4.6%); fusions were detected in 1.6% of patients and most frequently affected FGFR2, RET, ALK, FGFR3, and NTRK1/3. In patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma, the most frequently observed clinically informative genomic biomarkers occurred in KRAS (G12C, 1.6%; all others, 67.1%), BRCA1/2 (4.1%), BRAF (V600X, 1.5%), and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) (1.0%). In patients with colorectal cancer, the most common clinically relevant alterations were KRAS (49.0%), BRAF (V600E, 7.6%), and NRAS (5.7%) mutations; ERBB2 amplifications (2.5%); and MSI-H (1.8%). In patients with biliary tract cancers, actionable alterations included IDH1 mutations (11.1%), ERBB2 amplifications (4.6%), FGFR2 fusions (2.0%), MSI-H (2.0%), and BRAF V600E (1.5%). In patients with gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinomas, actionable alterations included ERBB2 amplifications (10.1%) and MSI-H (3.6%).

## Conclusion

Our data provide insight into the genomic landscape of patients with gastrointestinal cancers from the AME region using ctDNA analysis. These findings highlight the potential utility of liquid biopsy as a noninvasive tool for characterizing tumor genomic profiles and support its role in clinical practice.

## Full article

## Introduction

Advances in tumor sequencing have enabled unprecedented data-driven decision-making in clinical oncology for targeted or immunotherapies [1]. In the clinical setting, this requires incorporating a comprehensive evaluation of a gene panel and the capability to sequence tumors, potentially on multiple occasions, before making decisions about a new treatment plan. Tissue biopsies are commonly performed but are invasive and sometimes costly procedures that can be associated with complications [2]. A single tumor biopsy specimen might not capture the molecular heterogeneity of the tumor, influenced by random changes over time or specific treatment pressures [3–5]. Furthermore, the time needed to complete tissue acquisition and comprehensive testing may lead to delayed treatment decisions [6, 7].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as an appealing alternative due to its minimally invasive nature, easy repeatability, faster turnaround, and ability to provide spatially unbiased tumor characterization [8, 9]. Accordingly, several studies have used liquid biopsies to predict tumor response, resistance, prognosis, and recurrence in multiple tumor types [10].

Gastrointestinal cancers, including colorectal, pancreatic, esophageal, stomach, and hepatocellular cancer, account for over 25% of all cancer cases worldwide, and their prevalence continues to increase [11]. Approximately 35% of cancer-related mortality is attributed to gastrointestinal cancers [12]. Elucidation of the genomic landscape of such cancers can help understand their pathophysiology and identify both prognostic and predictive biomarkers for treatment response.

The tumor genomic profiles of patients with gastrointestinal cancers may be influenced by their ethnicity and geographical area of origin. Most studies profiling cancer genomics have been conducted in Europe and North America [13], with fewer genomic landscape data from Asia and the Middle East (AME). Furthermore, most existing evidence is based on tissue biopsy-based profiling, with few studies describing the experience with ctDNA NGS. Therefore, this study aimed to explicate the genomic landscape of ctDNA from patients with gastrointestinal cancers from the AME region using retrospective data collected in routine clinical practice.

## Methods

### Study Design

The study included data from patients with advanced gastrointestinal cancers seen in routine clinical practice in the AME region who were tested by Guardant360®. Tests were ordered between September 2016 and January 2023. Blood samples were collected from patients with advanced-stage pancreatic cancer, colorectal adenocarcinoma, biliary tract cancers, gastric or gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) adenocarcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and other gastrointestinal cancer types. The samples originated from Japan, Israel, Taiwan, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, India, and South Korea. Patients tested as part of prospective interventional clinical trials were excluded from the analysis.

### Circulating Tumor DNA Next Generation Sequencing

Guardant360 (Guardant Health Inc.; Redwood City, CA, USA) is a genomic profiling assay that comprehensively analyzes single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions, fusions, and amplifications (amps) in a blood sample [14, 15]. Guardant360 CDx was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2020 as the first blood-based genomic profiling assay [16] and is indicated for patients with advanced solid tumors both at diagnosis and in cases of disease progression. During the observation period, assay versions 2.9 to 2.12 were employed. These enabled the analysis of 70–83 genes and, from version 2.11 onward, assessed for microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status.

### Data and Statistical Analysis

This study focused on clinically informative genomic alterations for each tumor type as defined in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network® (NCCN) guidelines. Synonymous mutations and variants of unknown significance were excluded.

Sample characteristics, mutation frequencies, and alteration types were reported using descriptive statistics. Turnaround time was calculated from the day the sample was received in the lab to the day the report was issued. In most patients with multiple concurrent potential driver alterations, the somatic alteration with the highest variant allelic frequency (VAF) was accepted as the primary driver. Potential driver mutations in separate genes with similar VAFs were considered compound mutations.

For an expanded analysis of KRAS/NRAS mutations, “KRAS/NRAS Exon 2” indicates mutations in codon 12 or 13 of these genes. “Extended RAS” relates to mutations in exons 3 and 4 (codons 59, 61, 117, and 146). All other KRAS/NRAS mutations not currently included in standard guidelines, such as L19F, Q22K, D33E, and T50I, were defined as “atypical RAS.”

Plasma copy numbers for the ERBB2 and MET genes were analyzed as follows: ERBB2 copy numbers of 2.1–2.4, 2.5–4.0, and >4.0 were reported as +, ++, and +++ amplification, respectively. Similarly, MET copy numbers of 2.2–2.4, 2.5–4.0, and >4.0 were reported as +, ++, and +++ amplification, respectively.

### Database Analysis

The frequencies of NCCN-recommended genomic biomarkers in patients with pancreatic, colorectal, biliary tract, and gastric cancers were compared between the present cohort and databases publicly available in cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). The Memorial Sloan Kettering-Metastatic Events and Tropisms (MSK-MET) database is a pan-cancer, clinico-genomic database encompassing more than 25,000 patients, including primary and metastatic specimens from 50 tumor types [17]. The China Pan-Cancer (CPC) database [18] includes NGS data from tumor and blood specimens of more than 11,000 Chinese patients, encompassing 25 tumor types and over 100 tumor subtypes [18]. The frequencies of genomic alterations were described using descriptive statistics.

## Results

### Sample Characteristics

A total of 2,601 plasma samples were collected from 2,062 patients during the observation period. After excluding canceled tests (n = 72) and samples without detectable ctDNA (n = 292), 2,237 (86%) samples were included. Samples with only synonymous mutations (n = 36) and repeat samples from the same patient (n = 139) were further excluded, leaving 2,062 samples for analysis (Fig. 1a). The median test turnaround time was 7 days (mean, 8.1 days; range, 4–37 days). Patients’ median age was 63 years (range, 20–116 years). Females accounted for 42% of the study population. Samples originated from Japan (39.0%), Israel (12.0%), Southeast Asia (12.0%), Taiwan (11.0%), Middle East (11.0%), India (8.0%), and South Korea (7.0%) (online suppl. Fig. S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000545560).

Fig. 1.: a Characteristics of samples tested. ctDNA, circulating tumor DNA. b Cancer types profiled. BTC, billiary tract cancer; CCA, cholangiocarcinoma; GBC, gall bladder cancer; GEJ, gastro-esophageal junction.

The most prevalent malignancies represented were pancreatic cancer (33.0%), colorectal adenocarcinoma (31.0%), biliary tract cancer (BTC; cholangiocarcinoma, CCA or gallbladder cancer, GBC) (18.0%), gastric/gastro-esophageal (GEJ) adenocarcinoma (13.0%), and hepatocellular carcinoma (2.0%) (Fig. 1b). The median mutation count within each sample was 3 (range, 1–91). The median VAF was 1.4% (range, 0.02–95.1%).

#### Analysis of All Gastrointestinal Cancer Types

Among samples collected from patients with all gastrointestinal cancer types, the most frequently mutated genes were TP53 (70.4%), KRAS (44.0%), APC (25.7%), ATM (15.1%), and PIK3CA (12.3%) (Fig. 2a). Amplifications were most detected in EGFR (13.7%), CCNE1 (5.9%), PIK3CA (5.0%), MYC (4.7%), and FGFR1 (4.6%) (Fig. 2b). The prevalence of amplification in ERBB2 was 3.3%, with breakdowns as follows: +amp (11%), ++amp (22%), and +++amp (67%). For MET, the prevalence was 3.0%, with +amp at 18%, ++amp at 35%, and +++amp at 47%. The version of the assay that was available during the testing period did not distinguish between focal amplifications and aneuploidy of EGFR, CCNE1, CCND2, FGFR1, MYC, and PIK3CA, which may overstate these results. Fusions were detected in 1.6% of patients, most commonly involving FGFR2 (32.0%), RET (23.0%), ALK (18.0%), FGFR3 (12.0%), and NTRK1/3 (6.0%) (Fig. 2c). The most frequently observed fusion partners were TACC3 (12.0%), NCOA4 (12.0%), BICC1 (9.0%), STRN (9.0%), and EML4 (6.0%) (Fig. 2d). MSI-H status was observed in 1.0% of patients.

### Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma Analysis

This analysis included 682 patients. The most frequent mutations were observed in KRAS (69.2%), TP53 (68.4%), CDKN2A (14.7%), ATM (14.1%), and SMAD4 (13.0%) (online suppl. Fig. S2a). The most commonly amplified genes were EGFR (4.2%), CCNE1 (3.8%), KRAS (3.6%), FGFR1 (2.8%), and MYC (2.8%) (online suppl. Fig. S2b). The prevalence of ERBB2 amplification was 0.8%, comprising +amp (14%) and +++amp (86%). Two patients had ALK fusions (EML4-ALK and STRN-ALK). One each had an FGFR2 fusion (FGFR2-TXLNA) and NTRK1 fusion (TRIM33-NTRK1), with a cumulative actionable fusion frequency of 0.6%. The frequency of NCCN guideline-recommended biomarkers was also analyzed, with the most prevalent being KRAS mutations (excluding G12C, 67.1%), BRCA1/2 mutations (4.1%), KRAS G12C (1.6%), BRAF V600X (1.5%), and MSI-H (1.0%) (Fig. 3).

An in-depth analysis of modifications in the KRAS, BRCA1/2, BRAF, and ERBB2 genes was conducted in patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Among KRAS mutations, the most frequently observed variants were G12D (42.0%), G12V (30.0%), G12R (12.0%), Q61H (5.0%), and Q61R (3.0%) (Fig. 4a). The majority (88.0%) of KRAS mutations were in exon 2 (KRAS codon 12 or 13), whereas “extended” KRAS and “atypical” KRAS accounted for 10.0% and 2.0% of the KRAS mutated population, respectively (Fig. 4b). ERBB2 mutations were detected in 1.25% of the patients, with most involving the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) either as SNVs (75%) or exon 20 insertions (12.5%); mutations affecting the extracellular domain (ECD) were found in 12.5%. MET amplification was identified in 0.5% of patients, with +amp at 33% and ++amp at 67%. MET exon 14 skipping was detected in 1 patient.

The most frequently amplified genes were EGFR (21.9%), BRAF (8.4%), FGFR1 (7.5%), MYC (5.4%), and PIK3CA (5.3%) (shown in online suppl. Fig. S4b). Fusions were observed in 1.7% of patients and included RET (59.0%), ALK (25.0%), NTRK (8.0%), and MET (8.0%) (online suppl. Fig. S5). The detected relevant NCCN biomarkers included KRAS mutations (49.0%), BRAF V600E (7.6%), NRAS mutations (5.7%), ERBB2 amplifications (2.5%), and MSI-H (1.8%) (Fig. 5).

Only 6.0% of all KRAS mutations were G12C (shown in Fig. 6a). Within NRAS, frequent mutations included Q61K (30.0%), Q61L (22.0%), Q61R (18.0%), Q16H (11.0%), and G12D (7.0%) (shown in Fig. 6b). ### Biliary Tract Cancer Analysis

The biliary tract cancer analysis encompassed 369 patients, including 316 patients with cholangiocarcinoma and 53 patients with gallbladder cancer. Frequently mutated genes included TP53 (57.1%), KRAS (18.4%, including 1.1% G12C), ARID1A (14.9%), IDH1/2 (12.2%), and ATM (10.1%) (shown in online suppl. Fig. S8a). The most amplified genes were EGFR (9.0%), CCNE1 (6.7%), PIK3CA (5.2%), KRAS (4.9%), and MET (4.1%) (shown in online suppl. Fig. S8b). Fusions were detected in 3.8% of the samples, among which 50.0% affected FGFR2, 29.0% FGFR3, 14.0% RET, and 7.0% ROS1. The frequent fusion partners for FGFR2 were BICC1 (29.0%), ALAD (15.0%), KIAA1598 (14.0%), PLXNA1 (14.0%), SHRROOM3 (14.0%), and TACC2 (14.0%) (shown in online suppl. Fig. S8a, b). Among genomic biomarkers recommended for testing by the NCCN, the most frequent were IDH1 mutations (11.1%), ERBB2 amplifications (4.6%), FGFR2 fusions (2.0%), MSI-H (2.0%), and BRAF V600E (1.5%) (Fig. 7).

### Database Analysis

In pancreatic cancer, the overall frequency of KRAS mutations (other than G12C) was lower in the current cohort (67.1%) than in the MSK-MET database (82.1%) and CPC database (85.7%). However, the frequency of potentially actionable genomic alterations, including KRAS G12C, was similar across cohorts (Fig. 9a). In colorectal cancer, the frequencies of the NCCN-recommended biomarkers were similar across databases, with a notable difference in MSI-H frequency between the current dataset (1.8%) and the MSK database (9.0%) (Fig. 9b). In biliary tract cancer, the frequencies of alterations recommended for testing in IDH1 by the NCCN fell between those of the MSK-MET and CPC databases (Fig. 9c). In gastric cancer, the prevalence of ERRB2 amplifications was also between those reported in the MSK-MET and CPC databases, whereas MSI-H was observed only in the current cohort (Fig. 9d).

## Discussion

This analysis describes the genomic landscape of patients with advanced gastrointestinal cancers tested in routine clinical practice using a commercially available ctDNA NGS assay. Although it is likely that most patients were tested after receiving systemic therapy, alterations of guideline-recommended biomarkers were detected at the expected frequencies for all analyzed cancer types.

Analysis of biomarkers from tissue biopsy has traditionally been utilized in patients with gastrointestinal cancers. However, the acquisition and analysis of tumor specimens can be associated with various challenges, and liquid biopsy, as a noninvasive alternative, may overcome some of these limitations, particularly after disease progression [19]. Studies have found high agreement for the frequency of genomic biomarkers detected in tumor tissue and in ctDNA (80–90%) [8, 20, 21].

Moreover, NCCN guidelines recognize the role of liquid biopsy and ctDNA or cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis for gastrointestinal cancers. In pancreatic adenocarcinoma, cfDNA testing is recommended if tumor tissue testing is not feasible for biomarker analysis [22]. In biliary tract cancers, the guidelines recommend utilizing comprehensive molecular profiling for biomarker screening, including cfDNA testing, while acknowledging its limitation in detecting fusions depending on the panel used and the specific partner gene [23]. In the context of gastric cancer, NCCN suggests liquid biopsy testing using a validated NGS-based comprehensive genomic profiling assay performed in a CLIA-approved laboratory, particularly for patients unable to undergo traditional biopsy or for disease progression monitoring [24]. Concerning colon cancer, the guidelines acknowledge that NGS panels can identify rare and actionable genetic alterations and may be conducted using tissue- or blood-based (liquid) biopsy methods [25].

### Implications of the Pancreatic Cancer Analysis

Tissue samples from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma acquired through endoscopic ultrasound or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography may prove inadequate, often necessitating repeated invasive procedures. Comprehensive ctDNA NGS-based evaluation can offer an alternative for detecting guideline-recommended biomarkers for therapy selection in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer [26]. In a prior investigation, the identification of MSI-H through ctDNA testing showed strong agreement with tissue-based testing. It correlated with durable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), indicating that employing a thoroughly validated liquid biopsy assay could broaden the detection of MSI-H tumors in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. In the current study, the frequency for most actionable NCCN-recommended genomic biomarkers and KRAS G12C across different databases appeared similar. However, for non-G12C KRAS variants, the frequency was lower in the current cohort (67.1%) than in the MSK-MET (82.1%) and CPC (85.7%) databases. Mutations of KRAS can be detected in over 90% of patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma when tissue-based testing is employed [27]. Studies have shown high rates of agreement between ctDNA and tumor tissue for detecting KRAS mutations in other tumor types [8, 15, 28]. However, the disparity in detection rates between tissue and plasma-based analyses in pancreatic cancer has been observed in other studies [26, 29], with one study reporting a KRAS mutation detection rate in ctDNA below 50% [30]. Botrus et al. [26] demonstrated that in pancreatic cancer there is a difference in the frequency of detected alterations using ctDNA from samples collected at baseline (treatment-naïve), during treatment, and at disease progression, with KRAS mutations detected more frequently in baseline and progressed samples than in samples from patients undergoing treatment. Our analysis included a mixed cohort of patients tested in clinical practice, which could account for this observation. The biology of ctDNA might also play a role, as disease progression can manifest in diverse patterns. Tumor burden might also decrease following therapy, potentially affecting the quantity of shed tumor DNA.

#### Implications of the Colorectal Cancer Analysis

Despite guideline recommendations, incomplete genotyping is widespread and frequently leads to inappropriate treatment, resulting in a lack of benefit and premature disease progression in patients with advanced colorectal cancer. Various factors contribute to this under-genotyping, including the utilization of sequential single-biomarker testing approaches, inadequate tissue availability (estimated to impact 25% of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer), and shortcomings in tissue-based tests [8]. Furthermore, the prolonged duration associated with conventional tissue-based genotyping can result in patients initiating therapy before receiving biomarker results, thereby preventing their utilization in therapy selection. Commonly, archived tissue samples collected prior to treatment are evaluated for tumor biomarkers at the time of disease progression; however, such analyses fail to detect changes in actionable molecular profiles, which can occur in 20% of real-world cases [31]. ctDNA NGS has emerged as a viable alternative to tissue testing, overcoming many of these obstacles [8]. Several studies have shown the potential of liquid biopsies to serve as a treatment selection tool in advanced colorectal cancer [28, 32, 33]. Additionally, liquid biopsies are adept at capturing tumor heterogeneity, offering deeper insights into resistance mechanisms as gastrointestinal cancers progress [34]. This ability is particularly relevant for patients with advanced colorectal cancer who have experienced progression on EGFR monoclonal antibodies, as it can help uncover a range of resistance alterations, including mutations (BRAF, ERBB2, PIK3CA, PTEN, and ECD EGFR mutations), amplifications (such as ERBB2 and MET amplifications), and rare fusions (ALK, RET, and NTRK). Analysis of ctDNA NGS-based resistance mechanisms in patients from the BEACON trial unveiled several potentially actionable mechanisms in both experimental cohorts, notably MET amplifications observed in 19% and 17% of patients treated with doublet (encorafenib and cetuximab) and triplet (encorafenib, binimetinib, and cetuximab) regimens, respectively [35]. Moreover, emerging concepts like NeoRAS, the loss of KRAS and NRAS mutations after systemic treatment [36], and anti-EGFR therapy rechallenge for tumors that do not acquire RAS mutations [37] underscore the usefulness of comprehensive liquid biopsies in patients with advanced colorectal carcinoma.

In the current study, the prevalence of most of the guideline-recommended biomarkers was similar across cohorts, with a notable exception being MSI-H prevalence. Previous studies have demonstrated a high percentage agreement and positive predictive value of liquid biopsies in detecting MSI-H, including colorectal cancer [38, 39]. The current cohort’s prevalence rate of approximately 2% is also consistent with other prospective studies in colorectal cancer [8]. Furthermore, the study may also reflect patterns of real-world testing. Initial MSI-H or dMMR testing usually occurs prior to the first-line treatment. In contrast, liquid biopsies are more commonly utilized in later lines of treatment in clinical practice.

#### Implications of the Biliary Tract Cancer Analysis

Therapeutic opportunities for patients with biliary tract cancers have recently expanded, leading to several clinically relevant biomarkers, including FGFR2, IDH1, BRAF, NTRK, RET, ERBB2, and MSI-H. However, being a desmoplastic and often necrotic tumor, it poses a formidable challenge for tissue-based genomic profiling, with over a quarter of metastatic tumor biopsies failing to provide sufficient tumor content or DNA quality for NGS analysis [40]. Cholangiocarcinoma exhibits notable intra-tumoral heterogeneity, particularly at the tumor periphery, which can be overlooked when sampling from the central lesion alone. The ability to detect this heterogeneity is crucial, especially when evaluating tumors for resistance mechanisms (e.g., resistance to FGFR inhibitors often stems from sub-clonal populations carrying FGFR2 kinase domain mutations). Moreover, integrating noninvasive and promptly accessible genomic biomarker evaluation strategies could enhance patient management strategies. Previous studies have illustrated the utility of comprehensive ctDNA NGS in detecting guideline-recommended biomarkers with high correlation [41].

The current analysis showed similar findings, with the frequency of most of the recommended biomarkers being similar in different databases among the BTC cohorts. However, the frequency of IDH1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions was lower in the current and CPC cohorts compared to the North American MSK-MET database. Previous studies have noted comparable variations in the prevalence of these genes between Eastern and Western populations [42, 43].

### Implications of the Gastric/GEJ Adenocarcinoma Analysis

Studies have demonstrated the utility of ctDNA NGS profiling in gastric and GEJ tumors [44, 45]. The prevalence of NCCN-recommended biomarkers in gastric/GEJ tumors was similar across the databases in the current study. MSI-H was observed more frequently in the current cohort, while a higher incidence of ERBB2 amplification was observed in the MSK-MET cohort. There have been concerns about the correlation between ERBB2 copy number gain detected through NGS platforms analyzing tumor tissues or liquid biopsy and the ERBB2/HER2 (protein) overexpression or ERBB2 amplification observed via immunohistochemistry or FISH assays, respectively. A recent study in patients with ERBB2-positive breast cancer showed that in routine clinical practice, the agreement between ERBB2 amplification detected by NGS and ERBB2 positivity determined by FISH/IHC was approximately 70% [46]. The prevalence disparity in ERBB2 amplification may be regional, as we observed similar rates in CPC and the ctDNA AME cohort. Previous studies have reported that about 5–10% of patients with gastric or GEJ cancer are MSI-H, consistent with approximately 4% observed in the current cohort. MSI-H typically represents an inherent genetic characteristic of tumors rather than an acquired feature during cancer progression. However, in certain cases, treatment with ICIs can lead to changes in tumor microenvironment dynamics, potentially altering the MSI status. Some studies suggest that tumors initially classified as microsatellite stable (MSS) may acquire MSI-H status following treatment with ICIs, although such occurrences are relatively rare. As ICIs are approved for the treatment of gastric cancer, they may have been a factor in the ctDNA cohort, as ICIs are largely used in progressed patients in a real-world setting. Although rare actionable (and guideline-recommended) biomarkers were not observed in the current cohort, it is important to emphasize that they were tested (and found to lack relevant alterations) as a part of the comprehensive genomic profiling assay employed in this study.

### Genomic Databases

Most publicly available comprehensive databases for genomic information of patients with cancer are based predominantly on data from the United States. Notable examples include The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genomic Data Commons (GDC), which have been instrumental in advancing cancer research. Some European databases also exist, but access is often restricted due to regulatory or institutional policies. To our knowledge, no publicly available comprehensive databases cover cancer genomic data from the Middle East, Southeast Asia, or India. In China, some datasets are accessible through cBioPortal, while Japan has the C-CAT database, which requires an application and subscription fee. Each of these databases has its strengths and limitations regarding accessibility, patient diversity, and data comprehensiveness.

The lack of publicly available genomic databases in many regions highlights a significant gap in global cancer research. We have attempted to address certain aspects of this gap by publishing the ctDNA NGS landscape for the Middle East and North Africa [47], focusing on advanced solid tumors such as lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer. However, more can be done to consolidate genomic information in the region.

### Study Strengths and Limitations

The current study has several strengths. It presents large-scale genomic profiling data from comprehensive ctDNA NGS of patients with gastrointestinal cancers in the AME region. Moreover, the ctDNA-based methodology used in our study allowed for the evaluation of many genomic alterations from a single blood sample within a week, with findings consistent with those of other investigations from the region.

However, there are several limitations. This real-world investigation may have introduced biases in the profiles of tested patients, such as the predominance of patients who may have been tested after one or more lines of treatment for advanced cancer. Thus, the current cohort may not correspond to MSK-MET and CPC’s predominantly untreated advanced cancers. Additionally, since these databases are based on tissue samples and our study focuses on a liquid biopsy cohort, physician decisions regarding the selection of patients for liquid testing might limit the comparison of results. Furthermore, as patients’ testing and treatment histories were not known, cases with previously detected biomarkers may have been depleted, and the cohort may have been enriched for genomic alterations associated with resistance to targeted treatment. Therefore, the alteration frequencies observed in the current study may not fully represent those of all patients with gastrointestinal cancers from the AME region.

## Conclusion

Our study underscores the utility of liquid biopsies as a promising approach for genomic profiling in patients with advanced gastrointestinal cancers from the AME region. This research contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the efficacy of liquid biopsies and complements previous studies conducted in the region. Importantly, our findings reveal that the types and frequencies of genetic alterations identified in advanced gastrointestinal cancers of AME patients through liquid biopsies are similar to those detected in previously studied patient samples from Western countries. Comprehensive liquid biopsies can provide valuable insights into the molecular landscape of gastrointestinal cancers, paving the way for more personalized and effective treatment strategies tailored to the specific genomic profiles of patients.
