# Molecular characterization of colorectal cancer (CRC) using next generation sequencing (NGS) in bridging the gap between research and clinical practice: from biomarker discovery to clinical implementation

## Abstract

## Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most common cancer in men and third in females, a heterogeneous disease involving multistep mechanisms that represents 10% of all cancers globally. This study investigates gene mutation profiling in CRC using Next-Generation sequencing machine.

## Method

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues of 30 CRC patients were retrieved and reviewed. DNA was isolated from selected tissues. Desirable quality check using Qubit and Nanoquant machine was done, and desirable libraries prepared were loaded into the sequencer for sequencing. Using Illumina BaseSpace and Illumina Variant interpreter, generated FastQ data were treated for annotation, alignment, and mapping with reference genome. Sequencing-runs with Phred-score ≥ 30 were selected as desirable runs. Finally, the variants were validated on NCBI-dsSNP and Ensembl databases for clinical consequence interpretations.

## Results

Overall, patient distribution consists of 12(40%) females and 18 (60%) males with mean age (53.2 + 5.3). most patients were in TNM stage-3: 53.3% (15/30) and the least was Stage-4: 20%(6/30) respectively. Overall, 73.3%: (22/30) completed the sequencing, and 552 mutations involving 29 genes and 12 chromosomes were detected. The most upregulated variants are KIT:68(12.3%), FGFR4:61(11.1%), EGFR:60(10.9%), ALK:53(9.6%), DCUN1D1:41(7.4%), PDGFR:40(7.2%), KRAS:33(6.0%), CDK4:27(4.9%), FGFR3:26(4.7%), MTOR:14(2.6), while NRAS, CDK6, PIK3CA, and RET each has 13(2.4%) apiece. Chromosomes 4:134/55(24.2%), chr7:84/552(15.2%), chr12:71/552(12.9%), chr5:64/552(11.6%), chr2:61/552(11.1%), chr3:54/552(9.8%), and chr1:43/552(7.8%) are the most involved chromosomes. Nine genes (APC, NRAS, ALK, PIK3CA, KRAS, IDH1, FGFR1, ERBB2, and ESR1) are identified as pathogenic-causing variants in CRC.

## Conclusion

This is the first NGS-based molecular study on FFPE-CRC tissues in hospital-USM that showed the most upregulated variants in CRC and identified nine genes as crucial pathogenic variants.

## Full article

## Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a heterogeneous disease involving multistep and multi-molecular mechanisms. The biology of CRC is very complex, and till today, there is still no absolute known cause of CRC, hence the cause is still unknown [1, 2]. Globally, CRC is the second most common cancer in men and the third most common in females [3, 4]. CRC represents 10% of all cancers and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally [4]. In the US in 2023, CRC recorded approximately 153,020 cases from 1,958,310 new cancer cases [5]. In Europe, the rate varies country-wise because the data are not adequately updated in some nations due to poor screening and low economic status, which act as mitigating factors for adequate CRC data [6]. Although a low incidence rate was reported in Africa, this could also be associated with poor medical advancement and substandard clinical accessibility, leading to a lack of CRC screening strategy and/or data unavailability [7]. In Asia, the CRC ratio varies regionally with nations like China, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore recording higher rates than South and Southeast Asian countries [8, 9]. However, the present data on the frequency previously reported to rise in China, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore shows a falling figure due to regular early screening strategies invoked in the countries [10]. The contrast is the case in certain Southeast Asian such as Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia wherein the incidence is rising at an alarming rate, because of increased insatiate craving for a Westernized style of fatty diet, heightened sedentary daily lifestyle, and obesity [8, 11].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is an established health menace associated with poor treatment outcomes and poor prognosis [25, 26]. CRC is a disease commonly diagnosed at a late stage because affected patients usually present for consultation at a later stage of the disease, hence the attribution with high prevalence in late adulthood and elderly age [27]. In fact, as at the time of presentation of the patients in the healthcare centre, the presentations are very conspicuous especially bleeding per rectum and changes in bowel movement [28]. At this stage, the prognosis is poor perhaps because metastasis has already occurred, and palliative care might be the best and only therapy deemed suitable [29]. Interestingly, the incidence of CRC is decreasing in many advanced countries due to early screening in the form of molecular screening from routine national screening CRC programs put in place in these countries [30, 31]. However, the reverse is the case in developing and low-income countries [32, 33]. Following these findings, the race to identify crucial driver mutations for CRC early screening and precision medicine geared towards providing personalized targeted therapy for CRC patients has become the paramount goal of OMICs science and cancer research.

Next-generation sequencing machines are high throughput devices with accuracy above 99% for base calling accuracy and are used for molecular profiling of gene mutations in cancer [12]. It provides a guide on focus-targeted therapy in cancer patient management including colorectal cancer (CRC) by uncovering the genetic characterisation of the gene mutations commonly associated with cancer including CRC [13, 14]. The present study findings aim to provide comprehensive knowledge on the genetic changes or alterations at the genetic-epigenetic level in CRC tumourigenesis, by using NGS to provide biomarkers or driver gene mutations and explains the nucleotide substitutions and the eventual amino acid changes responsible for CRC propagation [15, 16]. This latter alteration causes functional loss of the specific protein due to the amino acid alteration, thus, causing unregulated differentiation of cells and increasing circulating tumours cells. This is the biogenesis of colorectal cancer progression.

There is a huge research gap in providing non-invasive early screening for cancer at the molecular level to boost the diagnosis of cancers such as CRC. This is a pilot study that looks at gene profiling among CRC cases in Hospital-USM to identify molecular markers that can be more accurately used to prognosticate CRC patient outcomes, and currently, there is no screening of CRC cases at a molecular level in our hospital. However, with the advent of NGS and its successful results, important driver mutations were identified as crucial gene mutations responsible for colorectal cancer propagation. The presence of the aberrated genes is used to provide precision medicine in the form of personalized therapy for CRC patients, as well as for monitoring therapy progress.

## Methodology

### Study design and participants recruitment

This is a prospective cross-sectional study, wherein patients were recruited using simple randomised sampling method based on the inclusion criteria employed for the study: patients with a diagnosis of CRC, with available FFPE tissue bloc and histopathology reports, have undergone surgery, and with medical records available in the hospital record unit. Because NGS is costly research and this is the first NGS study in the institution, the total cost of the research exceeded the original budget, hence, we did this pilot study with a small sample size to comprehensively know what the full cost at performing the research on a larger size would cost the institution. Hence the small sample of 30 which is suitable for pilot study based on the general flat rule which is to “use at least 30 subjects or greater to estimate a parameter” [17]

### Tissue samples and ethical statement

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks of 22 CRC tissues and 2 normal tissues were retrieved from the archived tissue bank of the pathology department of USM specialist hospital (Hospital-Pakar USM), Kelantan, Malaysia. The carefully chosen cases were reviewed by a pathologist using the Olympus BX43 light microscopy to identify the optimum section of the tumour prior to proceeding with nucleic acid (DNA) isolation and eventual NGS analysis. The tissue slides were deparaffinised and hydrated following standard protocols and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The tumour size, histopathological types, tumour grade, and TNM staging were obtained from the histopathology report Laboratory Information System (LIS) of the Pathology Department. Clinical data and other pertinent clinicopathological and demographic information of the patients were retrieved from the medical case notes and files of the patients from the medical records unit of the hospital. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee.

Universiti Sains Malaysia USM Human Research [Jawatankuasa Etika Penyelidikan Manusia USM (JEPeM)] in accordance with the [Hospital USM and Universiti Sains Malaysia research protocol guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the institution's (Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM)) human research ethics committee with an ethical approval code (USM/JEPem/21010076).

### Next-generation sequencing and library preparation

The FFPE tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm using the Leica rotation microtome, and genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from a 10-µm-thick tissue of 4 sections using QIAgen Advance DNA FFPE extraction kit by following the protocol guide. The isolated gDNA quality check was done using Qubit fluorometer machine for quantity (concentration) check while the purity level for the A260/280 (desirable range: 1.8–2.0) and A260/230 (desirable range: 2.0–2.2) absorbance ratio was checked using the Nanoquant machine. Libraries were prepared from the isolated DNA using Illumina AmpliSeq focus panel kit that detects 52 genes commonly known to be mutated in CRC such as KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA, PTEN, TP53, NRAS, EGFR genes, etc. Following the latter step, the quality check of the prepared libraries was done using the LapChip High Sensitivity (Thermo Fisher Scientific) machine. Libraries with desirable peaks between 150 and 400 base pairs were selected as satisfactory for pooling and loading into the sequencer machine. Sequencing was done using the Illumina MiniSeq machine. A successful and quality sequencing run was described as having a Phred score (Q-score) of ≥ 30, coverage depth varies within 30 or less to 500, and read length at > 50. Following the assertiveness of sequencing quality, sequenced data in the form of fastQ data was generated and treated for data cleansing. Here, data annotation, clustering and alignment were done, variant calling and filter criteria were applied to the Illumina base-space software. After this, variant calling and nucleotide changes as well as mutations were identified and analysed on the Illumina variant interpretation and statistical SPSS software respectively.

### Bioinformatic analysis

We employed Illumina BaseSpace software version 2.1.1 to treat our fastQ data to do the adapter trimming and cleansing, removal of low-quality sequence run with low Phred score [< Q-score of 30), short and ambiguous read depth (coverage depth < 50x–100x) [18]. The obtained cleaned sequenced reads were then processed for alignment and reference corresponding with the reference human genome (UCSC hg19; http://genome.ucsc.edu/) [19], and using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA)-MEM version 0.7.12-r1039 [20]. Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) IndelRealigner and BaseRecalibrator were adopted to remove duplicate reads, base quality score recalibration and indel realignment. Functional insights into genetic variation and somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertion deletions (Indels) calling were carried out for the tumour-normal samples using Illumina variant interpreter [21–23]. Furthermore, annotations and variants interpretation were performed using Illumina Annotation Engine version 2.0.11-o-g7fb24a09 and Pisces Variant caller version 5.2.9.23 respectively and were contrasted against CLINVAR, Franklin by Genoox, and NCBI online databases for mutation profiling and functional role (such as for frameshift insertion/deletion, synonymous/non-synonymous SNVs), mutation location (i.e. exonic, intronic, splicing, upstream, downstream, 3′and 5’ untranslated region [3’-UTR & 5′-UTR), amino acid alterations [AAs changes], allele occurrence proportions (based on 1000 Genomes Project, Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC). In the secondary analysis, Illumina’s BaseSpace Variant Interpreter (BSVI) version 2.17.0.60 software was employed to visualize the pertinent details of the variants detected to uncover the gene mutation location, types of mutation, nucleotide changes, amino acids substituted, and contrasted with already existing data on gene mutation profiling on publicly available online databases (https://www.cbioportal.org/mutation_mapper) [19].

### In silico pathogenic prediction and validation of the driver variants

Analysing the interspecific and functional regions of DNA is important to understand the homeostasis interplay of the cellular procedure in the entire human genetic-epigenetic system in tumourigenesis. This is because this region encodes the protein and nucleic acid in which the variants located might have pathogenic implications that affect the structure and functional role of the gene. In the present study, the In-Silico prediction model was employed to identify the clinical consequences of the variants and validated in NCBI-dsSNP and EnsembI. Using the In-Silico model to sort out intolerant variants from tolerant ones, we analysed all the identified amino acid changes associated with each variant and classify as tolerated, if the amino acid alteration is predicted compatible with normal protein’s function in the affected gene, or pathogenic if the prediction implies compromised protein function (compromise gene role). Then, we validate the detected variants by comparing them with data on the publicly available online databases of NCBI-dsSNP and EnsembI, which are well-known as reliable online databanks for mutation profiling of several mutation profiling for many cancer genetics. NGS output of our study’s gene mutation profiling of CRC was obtained via the Illumina MiniSeq sequencer machine to identify hotspot mutations considered pathogenic variants in CRC propagation.

### In-silico prediction of the pathogenic variants and validation with dbSNP and Ensembi databases

After filter criteria were applied to remove nuisance variants, unspecified variants and variants with a low run quality. The filter criteria settings are Quality-based filters of Phred quality score (Q-Score of ≥ 30), exclusion of base quality with a quality score below Q20, exclusion of short reads < 50 bp that may indicate sequencing errors or incomplete sequences, exclusion of unaligned reads that fail to map to the reference genome, exclusion of read depth < 50 × coverage, removal of variants with low-quality scores (QUAL < 20), and exclude all introns and unknown variants. A total of 105 variants involving 15 genes were identified as driver genes in colorectal cancer, by validating the mutation pattern identified in the present study’s CRC cases-NGS sequencing outcome with the online EnsembI & dbSNP NCIB database on CRC cases. Our outcomes revealed that based on the “In-Silico Prediction” used in our study for variant clinical consequence interpretation, nine genes are identified as pathogenic-causing variants in CRCs namely APC, NRAS, ALK, PIK3CA, KRAS, IDH1, FGFR1, ERBB2, and ESR1 genes. However, two genes [ERBB2 and ESR1] were classified as identified as “variant unspecified’’ in the databases.

APC variant is identified at position 112,175,530 in GRCh37 on chromosome 5, a frameshift indel deletion at coding location c4240 that resulted in an amino acid substitution from Valine (Val) to Termination (Ter) at protein position 1414, and was identified as deleterious/ damaging clinical implication in our study (Table 5).

NRAS variant is detected at position 115,258,774 in GRCh37 on chromosome 1, a missense mutation type at coding location c.8 and c.182 with amino acid substitution from glutamine (Glu) to alanine (Ala) at protein position-3 and from glutamic acid (Gln) to leucine (Leu) at protein position-61, both identified as deleterious/probably damaging clinical implications in our study (Table 5).

ALK variant is detected at position 29,443,625 in GRCh37 on chromosome 2, a missense mutation type at coding location c.3592 with amino acid substitution from Leucine (Leu) to Isoleucine (Ile) at protein position 1198 indicating a deleterious/probably damaging clinical implication in our study (Table 5).

PIK3CA mutation is detected at positions 178,936,091 and 178,936,095 in GRCh37 on chromosome 3, a missense mutation type at coding location c.1633 and c.1637 with amino acid substitution from glutamine (Glu) to Lysine (Lys) at protein position 545 and from glutamic acid (Gln) to Arginine (Arg) at protein position 547, both identified as deleterious/probably damaging clinical implication in our study (Table 5).

KRAS mutation is detected at positions 25,398,285 and 25,398,284 in GRCh37 on chromosome 12, a missense mutation type at coding location c.34 and c.35 with amino acid substitution from Glycine (Gly) to Serine (Ser) at protein position 12 and from Glycine (Gly) to Aspartate (Asp) at protein position 12, both identified as deleterious/probably damaging clinical in our study (Table 5).

IDH1 mutation was identified at position 209,113,113 in GRCh37 on chromosome 2, a missense mutation type at coding location c394 with amino acid substitution from Arginine (Arg) to Cysteine (Cys) at protein position 132 identified as deleterious-low confidence/possibly damaging clinical implication in our study (Table 5).

FGFR1 mutation was identified at position 38,282,215 in GRCh37 on chromosome 8, a missense mutation type at coding location 748 with amino acid substitution from Arginine (Arg) to Tryptophan (Trp) at protein position 250 and identified as deleterious/probably damaging clinical implication in our study (Table 5).

## Discussion

Our present research is the first in our institution (USM Specialist Hospital, Malaysia) to use NGS approach to comprehensively describe the hotspot gene mutation profiling in the FFPE tissue of patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer (CRC), following this, our study successfully identified prognostic and driver genes crucial in the screening and management of CRC. In addition, we identified mutation patterns concerning gene-chromosome specificity associated with CRC pathogenesis. Overall, from the total of 30 patients selected for this study, 60% were male and 40% were female. Histopathology-wise, late-stage level and TNM stage-3 were the most reported while moderately differentiated is the most identified tumour grade in the patients. This is consistent with global CRC data in most published articles, that CRC is more prevalent among males gender and the elderly age [34, 35], late tumour stages [36–39], and moderately and poorly differentiated grade [37, 40, 41].

From the total of 30 FFPE samples loaded into the NGS sequencing machine, 22 samples (73.3%) completed the run successfully. A total of 552 mutations involving 29 genes were detected. KIT, FGFR4, EGFR, ALK, DCUN1D1, PDGFR, KRAS, CDK4, FGFR3, and mTOR genes were the most mutated variants detected while AR, HRAS, CCND1, FGFR1, IDH1, and ESR1 were the least mutated. This is consistent with profiling reports from published papers in some regions [42–47]. Although the extent of the impact of gene aberrations on CRC patients is currently unknown, this made the quest for the identification of alteration in tumourigenesis of utmost importance and the need for personalized targeted therapies [48–50]. From this, clinical oncology is transitioning from a therapy paradigm expressed primarily by the structural site of tumourigenesis to a molecular characterisation driven by a critical role in guiding precision medicine. Gene aberration identification from mutation profiling is the fundamental yardstick for guiding the choice of personalised targeted therapy for cancers including CRC. From this knowledge, several targeted therapeutic agents and chemotherapeutics such as anti-EGFR, anti-VEGF/VEGFR, targeted anti-inhibitors like anti-BRAF, and anti-KRAS agents have been employed in combo with surgery with/without radiotherapy with a positive therapeutic prognosis reported [51–53].

In the present study, we successfully identify hotspots and prognostic genes crucial in the biogenetic process of CRC. Although there is no consensus on which mutation pipeline or profiling should be the landscape for the screening or diagnosing of a particular cancer [54–56]. Why? Because cancer is a complex heterogeneous disease with vast genetic-epigenetic factors playing a crucial role in the pathogenesis, and these factors differ from race and region [57]. Nonetheless, very common gene mutations have been reported as almost exclusively detected in certain cancers such as observed in colorectal cancer wherein mutations involving KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA, SMAD, APC, EGFR, and FGFGR are the most upregulated genes in the various studies [39, 42, 58–61]. In a study on gene mutational spectrum in South-eastern China on 117 FFPE CRC samples, KRAS, KIT, PIK3CA, MET and EGFR at frequencies 52.1%, 19.7%, 29.9%, 15.4% and 14.5% were the most upregulated hotspot variants [61]. The importance of the prognostic contribution of mutation profiling in targeted precision medicine was detailed in another study in China on 228 FFPE samples of CRC patients using NGS, wherein 4 genes (KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA and NRAS) were exclusively highly detected in CRC mutations and associated with poor overall survival OS, others include APC, SMAD, PDGFR, FGFR4 etc. [62]. Following this mutation discovery, targeted therapy in cancer treatment has greatly achieved positive progress in the treatment of cancers including CRC, such as the use of monoclonal antibody agents like cetuximab and panitumumab that target EGFR receptors to improve the survival of CRC patients [63].

Aside from the detailed driver genes in CRC, the present study also discovered the gene-chromosome specificity pattern associated with CRC biology. Here, it was shown that only 12 chromosomes were associated with the 552 aberrations detected by the NGS, and they include chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11,12, 17, and X, among which chr4: 24.3%(134/552), chr7: 15.2%(84/552), chr12: 12.9%(71/552), and chr5: 11.6%(64/552) are the most prevailing chromosomes involved in mutations in CRC. Furthermore, chromosome-gene specificity patterns were identified, indicating that certain gene mutations were exclusively detected on certain chromosomes, whether on the long or short arms. For example, chr12 harbours KRAS at > 95% specificity [64], EGFR on chr7 at 65–70% specificity [65, 66], APC gene mutation on chr5 at specificity at > 95% [67] and ERBB3 at about 35–40% specificity [68]. Worth mentioning are chromosomes with multi-gene specificity properties such as Chr3 which harbours DCUN1D1 at > 95% and PIK3CA at 30% specificity rate [69]. Likewise, chr7 which harbours 3 genes namely EGFR at > 95%, CDK6 & BRAF at 20–25% apiece specificity rate [70, 71], and chr4 with harbour KIT, PDGFR, and FGFR3 at > 95%, 70%, and > 60% specificity rate [72, 73].

Globally, colonoscopy remains the gold standard for CRC diagnosis, however, the disease is usually detected at a late stage perhaps because, the affected patients commonly present for consultation at a late stage when the disease presentation is very severe, conspicuous or metastasis has occurred [74, 75]. At this stage, treatment is palliative with chemotherapy but at this point, the treatment outcome is poor and the prognosis is worse. Through molecular profiling, non-invasive early screening using high throughput tools such as NGS provides hotspot genes that are high-risk driver genes for CRC diagnosis. The profiling pipeline discovered in the present study will ensure precision medicine by promoting personalized targeted therapy for the treatment of CRC patients especially at the late stage. In our study, we use the In-Silico prediction model to identify pathogenic genes and interpret their clinical implications, mutations involving nine genes are identified as pathogenic-causing variants in CRCs namely APC, NRAS, ALK, PIK3CA, KRAS, IDH1, FGFR1, ERBB2, and ESR1 genes. These gene mutations were also reported as pathogenic genes in some published articles [42–47]. Furthermore, we validated the aberrated genes identified in our CRC cases-NGS sequencing outcome with the online database of EnsembI and dbSNP-NCIB on CRC cases, all nine gene mutations were identified as pathogenic genes in CRC in the two databases.

In both online databases, KRAS, NRAS and BRAF mutations were exclusively identified as pathogenic in CRC, as RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway activators, their mutation is linked with poor therapeutic outcome and poor prognosis [76]. Adenomatous Polyposis Coli (APC) mutations in both databases are reported as highly expressed in CRC due to β-catenin build-up that leads to uncontrollable cell division and colorectal cancer growth. The latter pathogenic effects lead to the development of targeted agents against APC gene mutation [77, 78]. The presence of FGFR1 mutations through the JAK/STAT pathway in CRC is not very common but when present can be an indication of more aggressive CRC, especially at the advanced stage and its pathogenic effect is linked with chemotherapy and anti-EGFR treatment failure in CRC [79]. Generally, IDH1 mutation is less common in CRC and is one of the rarest mutations in CRC, their mode of pathogenicity is via accumulation of an oncometabolite (2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG)) that promotes CRC progress by causing wide changes through epigenetic dysregulation and tumour proliferation [80, 81]. Their exact role in CRC progress is still limited [82], however, they identify to enhance CRC growth and metastasis thus, promoting aggressive CRC character [83]. Also, the prognosis outcome of IDH1 is still less documented [84]. The pathogenicity expression of PIK3CA gene mutation was reported at 15% in CRC patients [85], mostly at the proximal part of the colon in CRC [86, 87]. Its pathogenic impact supports tumour growth and treatment failure [88], and several ongoing trials are crucially investigating the therapeutic role of PIK3CA inhibition as a possible therapeutic agent for CRC treatment.

ESR1 mutations are commonly associated with tumourigenesis in reproductive organs and are not frequently detected in CRC. They are more regularly related to hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, hence, their role in CRC is less significantly documented [89]. ESR1 mutations are reported as hormone-initiated cancers that act via the ligand-binding receptors “ERα (ESR1) and ERβ (ESR2)” domain of the estrogen signalling pathway to actively stimulate estrogen receptors to become active despite the absence of estrogen [90]. Usually, ERβ is the most expressed estrogen receptor in both normal and cancerous colon epithelium, however, ESR1 mutation via the ERβ cascade is lost in CRC, signifying the pathogenic role of estrogen-signalling involvement in CRC propagation. Following this latter fact, estrogen is reported to show anti-tumour properties by specifically initiating pro-apoptotic-mediated signalling via ERß and regulation of the tumour microenvironment [90]. Hence, its role as a potential therapeutic avenue in CRC.

## Conclusion, limitations and recommendations

NGS has transformed CRC studies and diagnostics by providing information on gene mutation profiling of CRC to improve precision medicine and prognosis. Our study being the first to use NGS to investigate hotspots gene mutations in CRC for use as non-invasive screening and focus-guided therapies in CRC management, the present study identified nine genes as exclusively pathogenic mutations in CRC. Also, patterns and degrees of the gene-chromosome specificity exclusively observable in CRC pathogenesis were described.

Despite the success of our study, there are some limitations encountered which involve the small sample sizes that prevented wide coverage of pipeline profiling and wider comparison with other data. Also, the difficulty isolating high-quality DNA from the FFPE tissues is a worrisome stage. The inability to extract a DNA sample from the FFPE tissues with acceptable absorbance ratios of A260/280 and A260/230 between 1.8–2.0 and 2.0–2.2 respectfully is a major problem. Lastly, the financial constraint due to limited budget prevented the researchers from further optimization. The cost of carrying out NGS-based research in genomics is a very crucial and setback factor because it reduces the potential capability for performing expanded all-inclusive research.

As our institution's first study on molecular gene mutation profiling of CRC-FFPE tissues using NGS, and based on our overall expertise during this research, we advocate for more extensive mutation detection to investigate a greater variety of genetic abnormalities (like SNVs, indels, and CNVs) in a larger sample size involving various specimens like blood, fresh tissues, and FFPE for a definitive comparison and profiling result. This will boost personalized targeted chemotherapy used once the most upregulated aberrated genes are identified and statistically confirmed in the most reliable databases such as NCBI, Franklin by Genoox or Varsome databases.
