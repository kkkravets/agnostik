Phase II Single-Arm Study of Palbociclib and Cetuximab Rechallenge in Patients with KRAS/NRAS/BRAF Wild-Type Colorectal Cancer

Jonathan D Sorah, Dominic T Moore, Matthew J Reilley, Mohamed E Salem, Tammy Triglianos, Hanna K Sanoff, Autumn J McRee, Michael S Lee
The Oncologist

Abstract

Background

Cetuximab is often administered to patients with KRAS wild-type (KRAS-WT) metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), although resistance inevitably develops. We hypothesized that co-inhibition of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) with cetuximab and downstream cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK) 4/6 with palbociclib would be effective for anti-EGFR rechallenge in KRAS-WT mCRC.

Methods

We designed a single-arm, Simon’s 2-stage, phase II trial of cetuximab and palbociclib in KRAS-WT mCRC treated with ≥2 prior lines of therapy. We report here on cohort B rechallenging patients with anti-EGFR-based therapy who had disease control of at least 4 months on prior anti-EGFR therapy. Primary endpoint was disease control rate (DCR) at 4 months.

Results

Ten evaluable patients were enrolled in this cohort. The 4-month DCR was 20%, which did not fulfill the prespecified 4-month DCR rate to continue. Median progression-free survival was 1.8 months and median overall survival was 6.6 months. Three patients had stable disease, although overall response rate was 0%. Most common treatment-related grades 3-4 adverse events were lymphopenia and leukopenia.

Conclusion

Selection of patients for anti-EGFR rechallenge using clinical criteria alone was insufficient to identify response to palbociclib + cetuximab. Additional biomarkers are needed to select anti-EGFR rechallenge and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis is planned for samples collected in this study. (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03446157)

  • The combination of cetuximab and palbociclib, although well tolerated, failed to show anti-tumor activity in patients with KRAS-WT mCRC who were selected solely based on previous response to anti-EGFR therapy.

  • The study emphasizes the need for further biomarkers to determine possible mechanisms of resistance and therapeutic options such as circulating tumor DNA.

Discussion

Cetuximab, an anti-EGFR antibody, is standardly used in patients with KRAS-WT mCRC alone or in combination with chemotherapy.1 However, resistance mechanisms inevitably develop, usually through aberrant activation or alternative signaling of effectors in the MEK-ERK pathway.2,3 Reactivation of mitogenic MEK-ERK pathways causes increased expression of cyclin D, which then interacts with CDK4/6 to drive cell cycle progression. To overcome this resistance mechanism, there is a strong rationale for combining anti-EGFR therapy with CDK 4/6 inhibitors like palbociclib. We hypothesized that addition of palbociclib to cetuximab in patients with KRAS-WT mCRC would lead to clinically significant cancer control and tested this in both anti-EGFR therapy naïve patients (cohort A) and those with cancer control for at least 4 months on anti-EGFR therapy (cohort B, reported here).

This phase II single-arm Simon’s 2 stage study showed a 4-month DCR of 2/10 (20%) patients. To justify further enrollment, our study required 5 patients to achieve 4 months of disease control. Consequently, the study was halted for futility after the first stage. The 2 patients who achieved at least 4 months of disease control had clinically meaningful duration of disease control of 5.5 and 24.7 months, respectively. The best response for 3 patients was stable disease, while 6 had progressive disease and 1 was removed from the study due to a non-healing necrotic pelvic lesion. Figure 1 demonstrates each patient’s disease control. Median overall survival, a secondary endpoint, was 6.6 months.

This study demonstrates that clinical criteria alone are insufficient to identify response to rechallenge cetuximab combined with palbociclib in this patient population. Prior studies did suggest that patients selected for rechallenge based on clinical parameters such as prior response to anti-EGFR therapy and sufficient intervening duration of washout from anti-EGFR therapy could benefit from anti-EGFR rechallenge.4 However, resistant subclones harboring resistance mutations in KRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1, or the EGFR ectodomain emerge under the selective pressure of anti-EGFR therapy, and ctDNA evidence of these subclones can be detected prior to radiographic and clinical progression.3,5-7 Data increasingly demonstrate that EGFR-antibody rechallenge is most likely to be beneficial in patients who lack resistant subclones detectable by ctDNA.8,9 Notably in the phase II single-arm CHRONOS trial, 36 individuals with prior EGFR-inhibitor treatment who retained KRAS-WT status via ctDNA at screening and progressed after an anti-EGFR-free regimen all received panitumumab monotherapy. In a preliminary report, there were 8 partial responses (ORR 30%) and a disease control rate of 59% of patients.10 In conjunction with receiving panitumumab, ctDNA was collected from each participant throughout treatment to facilitate mutation analyses of possible resistance mechanisms. Correlative studies are ongoing. We also await data from cohort A in our study: anti-EGFR therapy naïve patients.

Assessment, Analysis, and Discussion

Colorectal cancer is a common disease in the US, with approximately 150 000 new diagnoses annually. It is also a lethal disease, remaining the third most common cause of cancer-related death in women and second in men nationally.11

Approximately 40% of patients with mCRC are wild type in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF, and thus are candidates to receive anti-EGFR therapy such as cetuximab or panitumumab, typically administered either in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy or as monotherapy. Nevertheless, the duration of response is limited, and eventually resistance to anti-EGFR therapy does develop. The response rate with single-agent cetuximab or panitumumab is 20-22% and median PFS is 4.1-4.4 months.12 Thus, novel therapies to address acquired resistance to anti-EGFR therapies are needed.

Although a wide variety of anti-EGFR resistance mechanisms have been identified, they generally result in reactivation of ERK signaling and its downstream effector pathways.2,3 Thus, therapies that target downstream of EGFR in the ERK pathway may synergize with anti-EGFR therapy and be more beneficial in cases of acquired resistance. ERK reactivation increases expression of cyclin D1, which then binds to and activates CDK4/6, enabling cell cycle progression through the G1-S checkpoint.3 Gene expression pathway analyses demonstrate high levels of cell cycle pathway activation in CRCs,13 and combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with MAPK pathway inhibition has shown activity in a range of preclinical models.14-17 Therefore, we hypothesized that addition of palbociclib to cetuximab as rechallenge treatment would provide further clinical benefit with a clinically meaningful 4-month disease control rate. This combination of palbociclib 125 mg daily days 1-21 of a 28-day cycle along with cetuximab 400 mg/m2 on day 1 followed by 250 mg/m2 weekly was determined to be the recommended phase II dose in a prior phase I study performed in head and neck squamous cell cancer. The combination was well tolerated, with the only grades 3-4 toxicities reported due to myelosuppression including anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia.18

This report describes the results from the cohort of patients who were rechallenged with cetuximab plus palbociclib. Patients were required to have clinical features suggestive of improved likelihood of response to anti-EGFR rechallenge, with at least 4 months of disease control on prior anti-EGFR therapy and last anti-EGFR therapy at least 8 weeks prior to trial initiation. Prior studies have shown that resistant subclones recede with time off of anti-EGFR therapy as they lose the selective advantage they enjoy during anti-EGFR therapy, with a median half-life of 4.3-4.4 months.19

The primary endpoint of 4-month disease control rate (DCR) was not met at prespecified interim analysis and enrollment was halted for futility after the first stage of the 2-stage study for the rechallenge cohort; however, this trial raises several important issues. First, 2 patients on trial had a DCR of at least 4 months with rates of 5.5 and 24.7 months, respectively. Thus, patients who can be selected for rechallenge-based strategies, particularly with novel combinations, are likely to derive significant benefit, although the clinical criteria used for selection in our study were insufficient to predict for potential response. It is possible that more stringent clinical criteria, such as prior radiographic response rather than simply disease control, or a longer duration of washout after prior anti-EGFR therapy, may better predict for responsiveness to anti-EGFR rechallenge. Additionally, this study underscores the importance of ctDNA as a potential biomarker for the likelihood of benefit with anti-EGFR rechallenge based therapy. Correlative studies are ongoing using ctDNA to determine whether the patients who had rapid progression on cetuximab plus palbociclib indeed harbored detectable resistant subclones.

Given the pervasiveness and lethality of mCRC, new therapies and combinations of therapies are urgently needed. This study explores a novel combination therapy to bypass anti-EGFR resistance if appropriate patient population is selected. Though this study did not meet its primary endpoint, there were some patients who derived substantial clinical benefit, underscoring the need for ctDNA analyses to better predict likelihood of benefit with anti-EGFR therapy rechallenge. As a part of this study, ctDNA was collected from each patient and is undergoing analysis currently to determine any resistance mechanisms to anti-EGFR therapy. The role of the addition of palbociclib to cetuximab in rechallenge remains unclear, although it is likely that palbociclib addition was insufficient to result in clinical benefit in patients who were already resistant to anti-EGFR therapy. Results from cohort A of this study in which anti-EGFR naïve patients received cetuximab plus palbociclib will better demonstrate if CDK 4/6 inhibition can improve outcomes when combined with cetuximab.
