Molecular characterization of adenocarcinomas arising in the urinary bladder following augmentation cystoplasty: a multi-institutional study✩

Bradley A. Stohr, Emily Chan, Joshua A. Anderson, Andres Matoso, Belkiss I. Murati Amador, Liang Cheng, Adeboye O. Osunkoya
Human pathology

Abstract

Development of malignancy is a rare complication following augmentation cystoplasty, and the majority of tumors observed in this setting are adenocarcinomas. Here, we sought to genetically profile these tumors by targeted DNA sequencing of a multi-institutional cohort of adenocarcinomas that developed in the urinary bladder following augmentation cystoplasty. Carcinomas arising in the urinary bladder of patients with history of augmentation cystoplasty were obtained from 4 major academic institutions, with cases diagnosed as urothelial carcinoma excluded from the study. The cases were analyzed using a DNA sequencing panel that includes 529 genes and genome-wide copy number assessment. The most frequently altered genes included TP53, KRAS, and MYC, and the vast majority of cases demonstrated mutational profiles consistent with gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas. One case demonstrated an EML4::ALK fusion together with an MSH3 frameshift mutation and hypermutated phenotype, characteristic of a rare but aggressive subtype of colorectal adenocarcinoma that may benefit from targeted ALK inhibition therapy. Importantly, six other tumors in the cohort also had potentially targetable molecular alterations, involving ATM (2 cases), BRCA1 (2 cases), EGFR (1 case), and ERBB2 (1 case). To our knowledge, this study represents the most comprehensive molecular characterization to date of adenocarcinomas arising in the urinary bladder following augmentation cystoplasty. Despite the unique environment of the augmented tissue, the resulting tumors demonstrate a spectrum of driver mutations similar to that of primary gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas. Importantly, molecular alterations potentially amenable to targeted therapy were identified in the majority of cases.

Introduction

Augmentation cystoplasty is a surgical procedure that increases bladder capacity and compliance by anastomosis of the bladder with a gastrointestinal tissue graft from the stomach, ileum, or colon [1]. The procedure can be used to treat patients with refractory neurogenic bladder dysfunction. Augmentation cystoplasty is associated with a risk of subsequent urothelial carcinoma or adenocarcinoma of the bladder, with adenocarcinomas being the most frequent tumor type seen in this setting [2]. These tumors are often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with frequent lymph node involvement and distant metastases.

We recently described the clinicopathologic features of ten non-urothelial carcinomas arising after augmentation cystoplasty [3]. Here, we report the molecular characterization of these ten cases, with a focus on targetable alterations identified in this aggressive tumor cohort.

Material and methods

Bladder carcinomas in patients with history of augmentation cystoplasty were obtained from four major academic medical centers in the United States [3], with cases diagnosed as urothelial carcinoma excluded from the study. Genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissue, and tumor-only sequencing was performed using a clinically validated capture-based assay as previously described [4]. In brief, the sequencing assay was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (San Diego, CA, USA) and targets the coding regions of 529 cancer-related genes, including select introns from 47 genes and the TERT promoter, with a total sequencing footprint of 2.8 Mb. Also included in the assay are 2000 unique sequences encompassing common single nucleotide polymorphisms to facilitate genome-wide copy number analysis. Single nucleotide variants and indels were verified using the Integrative Genomics Viewer, genome-wide copy number was analyzed using Nexus Copy Number (Biodiscovery, Hawthorne, CA), and Delly was used for detection of structural variants [5]. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic molecular alterations were manually classified by an experienced molecular pathologist based on data from cBioportal for Cancer Genomics (www.cbioportal.com), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), and PubMed (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/). Only variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic are reported in the results section. The study was completed following the guidelines of and with approval from our institutional review boards (including UCSF Human Research Protection Program Institutional Review Board, IRB#: 15-16090).

Results

Representative histologic images are shown in Fig. 1, while a summary of the morphologic and molecular findings from all ten cases is shown in Fig. 2. Details of pathogenic and likely pathogenic molecular alterations for the cases are shown in Supplemental Table 1.

The clinicopathologic features of the cases from this study are outlined in greater detail in our previous publication on this cohort [3], with a summary of those findings included here. At the time of cancer diagnosis, the patients ranged in age from 27 to 87 years, with a mean age at diagnosis of 47. In most of the cases, the precise date of augment surgery was uncertain, but in the two cases with known augment surgery date, the cancer diagnosis was 24 (Case 3) and 15 (Case 9) years after the augment surgery. Such a delayed presentation is typical of these tumors, as a recent review of 16 such cases showed a median latency period of 20 years [2]. In all of the cases from our cohort that underwent cystectomy and node dissection (Cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, and 9), the tumor appeared to originate from the grafted augment tissue adjacent to the anastomosis (with associated glandular dysplasia in 3 of the cases), and extension into the perivesical fat and nodal involvement was identified.

At least partial treatment and follow-up information was available for 9 of 10 patients in the cohort. Three patients (Cases 2, 3, and 4) were treated with cystectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy, four patients (Cases 1, 6, 8, and 9) were treated with cystectomy without known adjuvant chemotherapy, and two patients (Cases 6 and 11) were treated with chemotherapy following transurethral resection of tumor. To our knowledge, none of the patients received targeted therapeutics. As expected based on the advanced stage of most of the cases, clinical outcomes were typically poor. Three patients (Cases 3, 5, and 10) died of disease within 1–19 months of surgery, and two other patients (Cases 1 and 9) were discharged to hospice within 7 and 4 months of surgery, respectively. Two patients (Cases 2 and 4) are alive with disease as of last clinical follow-up 12 months and 37 months after surgery, respectively. Three patients (Cases 7, 8, and 9) were lost to follow-up.

Nine of the ten cases demonstrate morphologic and molecular features supporting an adenocarcinoma diagnosis. In contrast, case 5 – originally diagnosed as a large cell undifferentiated carcinoma – demonstrates a molecular profile that strongly supports urothelial rather than gastrointestinal origin. TERT promoter mutations are one of the most common alterations in urothelial carcinoma but very rare in gastric adenocarcinoma [6–8], and STAG2, KMT2C, and KMT2D mutations are also more common in urothelial carcinoma than gastric adenocarcinoma based on data in the cBioportal for Cancer Genomics database. Of note, this is the only patient in the cohort with a prior history of urothelial carcinoma in situ, suggesting that the invasive large cell undifferentiated carcinoma represented progression of the previously diagnosed urothelial carcinoma rather than an augment-derived tumor.

Among the nine adenocarcinomas profiled, a spectrum of driver mutations typical of gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma is seen, including KRAS alterations (6 cases), an APC alteration (1 case), and a SMAD4 alteration (1 case). Other common alterations identified in the adenocarcinoma cohort include TP53 mutations (7 cases) and MYC amplification (3 cases). Notably, one of the tumors in a patient with colon graft demonstrates a molecular profile typical of a rare but aggressive subtype of colorectal adenocarcinoma, with an EML4::ALK fusion, MSH3 frameshift mutation, and hypermutated phenotype (as demonstrated by 95 additional mutations detected in the tumor (data not shown)) [9]. Of note, this patient subsequently developed rectal adenocarcinoma, but due to the different morphologies and immunoprofiles, the initial tumor in the bladder and the subsequent adenocarcinoma in the rectum were favored to represent separate primaries [3].

Of the ten tumors profiled in this study, seven demonstrate potentially targetable molecular alterations, including ATM truncating mutations (2 cases), BRCA1 inactivating mutations (2 cases), EGFR amplification (1 case), ERBB2 amplification (1 case), and EML4::ALK fusion (1 case).

Discussion

Carcinomas of the urinary bladder arising in the setting of augmentation cystoplasty are typically aggressive tumors with poor clinical outcomes. A recent retrospective study describing 16 patients with such tumors documented a high rate of metastases and a low 1-year survival rate [2], and all six patients in our cohort who underwent cystectomy also demonstrated nodal metastases [3].

Given the aggressive nature of these tumors, it is notable that DNA sequencing identified potentially targetable molecular alterations in seven of the ten tumors analyzed here. Four of the tumors demonstrated mutations in BRCA1 or ATM, alterations that disrupt DNA repair mechanisms and may confer susceptibility to poly (ADP ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [10,11]. Copy number analysis revealed receptor tyrosine kinase amplification in two tumors (EGFR amplification in one and ERBB2 amplification in the other), and tumors with amplification of these receptors may benefit from targeted inhibitor therapy [12,13]. Finally, one tumor demonstrated an EML4::ALK fusion seen in an aggressive subset of colorectal adenocarcinomas [9], with a recent study showing potential benefit from ALK inhibition in one such case [14]. Of note, a recent study that sequenced four bladder adenocarcinomas in patients with history of augmentation cystoplasty did not identify a similarly high level of potentially targetable molecular alterations [2]. However, that study used a sequencing panel covering select exons of 21 genes, in contrast to the more comprehensive sequencing panel used in the present study that encompasses 529 genes and includes copy number analysis.

The design of this study excluded cases diagnosed as urothelial carcinoma, and as a result, the vast majority of cases are adenocarcinomas. One case (case 5) lacked the morphologic features of adenocarcinoma and instead showed large cell undifferentiated morphology. While the morphologic features of this tumor were not specific for a particular site of origin, the molecular profile provided very strong support for a diagnosis of poorly differentiated urothelial carcinoma. Thus, in addition to identifying potential therapeutic targets, targeted sequencing can be useful in establishing a more definitive diagnosis in cases that are high-grade with nonspecific morphologic features.

The grafted gastrointestinal tissues are exposed to a unique environment, which might promote tumor initiation through molecular pathways different from those occurring in the native tissue locations. However, the adenocarcinomas in this study appear to demonstrate a spectrum of mutations similar to that of primary gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas [15–17]. Due to the rarity of carcinomas in the setting of augmentation cystoplasty, our cohort is necessarily small, and it is possible that sequencing of a larger number of cases might reveal differences in the mutational profiles of tumors arising from gastrointestinal tissues in augment sites versus native sites.
