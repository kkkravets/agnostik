Comprehensive analysis of PIK3CA mutations in advanced colorectal cancer using circulating tumor DNA profiling

Allante Milsap, Jill Tsai, Baqir Jafry, Alex Kim, Syed Mohammad Ali Kazmi, Nilesh Verma
NPJ Precision Oncology

Abstract

Mutations in PIK3CA are prevalent in advanced colorectal cancer (CRC) and are associated with advanced-stage disease and poor prognosis. However, the prognostic value of these mutations remains unclear. To better understand their role in CRC progression, we evaluated the genomic landscape of PIK3CA mutations (PIK3CAm) in advanced CRC using plasma-based ctDNA from ~17,000 samples (Guardant360 database). Mutation co-occurrence, MSI-H status, TMB scores, and exon-specific mutation frequencies were analyzed. Our study shows that the frequency of PIK3CAm (19.2%) and distribution of exons 9 and 20 are similar to those found previously in the literature. Our results demonstrate PIK3CAm having a low frequency of MSI-H co-occurrence, higher TMB scores, and increased co-occurring alterations in APC, BRAF, EGFR, ERBB2 and KRAS, which may suggest higher genomic instability. Our findings underscore the clinical significance of PIK3CAm in CRC and supports the development of tailored therapeutic strategies targeting this mutation.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant public health burden in the United States, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with an estimated 154,000 new cases and 53,010 fatalities projected in 20241. Nearly half of CRC patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage or develop metastasis, which carries a 5-year survival rate of less than 16%2,3. The introduction of targeted therapies has driven improvements in prognostic outcomes for metastatic CRC (mCRC), nearly doubling overall survival compared to traditional chemotherapy alone4,5. However, their efficacy is constrained by narrow therapeutic windows a limited understanding of actionable biomarkers4,6, underscoring the need for further genetic characterization of CRC.

PIK3CA mutations (PIK3CAm) are found in 10–20% of CRC cases yet have poorly understood clinical implications7–9. Regarding PIK3CA mutations according to the patient’s stage of colorectal cancer, the TCGA cohort and DFCI cohort included patients across stages. Stage I to III patents had a prevalence of PIK3CA mutations of 25.7% and 21.7% in TCGA and DFCI cohorts, respectively10. PIK3CA encodes p110α, the catalytic subunit of class IA phosphatidylinositol 3-kinases (PI3Ks). Class IA PI3Ks are heterodimers that consist of a catalytic subunit (p110α, β, or δ, which names the resulting PI3K complex) and a regulatory subunit (p85α, p85β, p55α, p55γ, or p50α)11. In quiescent cells, p110α is inhibited by p85. This inhibitory effect is relieved as activated receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) bind to the SH2 domains on p85, resulting in PI3K activation. Once activated, PI3K translocates to the cell membrane and converts phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which in turn activates AKT. AKT phosphorylates mTOR and other mediators that govern cell survival, growth, and migration12–14. In CRC, mutations that stimulate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway are key drivers of tumor progression. By enabling constitutive PI3K activation irrespective of upstream signaling, PIK3CAm significantly enhance CRC cell proliferation15.

Over 80% of PIK3CAm occur in exon 9 (codons 542 and 545), which codes the helicase domain, or in exon 20 (codon 1047), which codes the kinase domain16. Discerning the mutation by gene domain holds clinical significance, given that several investigations suggest that exon 20 mutations are associated with resistance to anti-EGFR therapy, in contrast to mutations in exon 9 or other locations17. PIK3CAm commonly occur in several cancers, including breast, lung, ovarian, and gastric16,18. Their role is most defined in breast cancer, where there are two FDA approved targeted therapies for patients with advanced breast cancer and PIK3CAm [FDA Alpelisib]19. In CRC, however, the prognostic value of PIK3CAm remains controversial. Some studies suggest a link between PIK3CAm and overall survival (OS), while others show no significant association8,10,20. Additionally, PIK3CAm frequently co-occur with KRAS mutations8–10,20 although their relationship with other mutations, such as BRAF, remains less consistent10,20.

This retrospective study aims to evaluate the genomic landscape of PIK3CAm, including their co-occurrence with other genetic mutations, in advanced colorectal adenocarcinoma using plasma-based next generation sequencing of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). While previous studies have explored PIK3CA mutations, our study adds value by using ctDNA, which offers a more comprehensive assessment of the entire tumor burden, avoiding the limitations of single biopsy sites. Additionally, we have investigated associations with MSI-H, TMB scores, and the potential prognostic implications of PIK3CA mutations, areas that remain understudied in colorectal cancer.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective analysis of deidentified, adult patients with advanced CRC tested with a next generation sequencing assay called Guardant360©. Guardant360 is a plasma-based “liquid biopsy,” which sequences ctDNA and reports on single nucleotide variants, insertions/deletions, amplifications, and fusions on up to 83 genes with additional reporting on microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational burden (TMB)21,22.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board (Pro00034566), with strict measures in place to ensure patient confidentiality throughout. This study utilized a de-identified, publicly available database; therefore, informed consent was not required according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, as the data could not be linked back to individual participants. This was approved by the relevant IRB committees.

Cohort description

Samples were assessed in two cohorts. The first cohort included patients with ≥1 alteration detected between October 2020–December 2022 (17,622 samples). These samples were assessed via cBioPortal23–25. The demographics analysis assessed sex and age. PIK3CAm mutations were assessed for frequency and distribution.

Comparative analysis of PIK3CAm and PIK3CA-ND

For further analyses, samples with ≥1 PIK3CAm were grouped and compared to those with PIK3CA not detected (PIK3CA-ND). These groups were compared for number of alterations per sample with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. PIK3CAm vs. PIK3CA-ND were also assessed for frequency of co-occurrence for all genes reported on the assay with a two-sided Fisher’s exact test (p-value) and false discovery rate (q-value). Blood TMB values were compared using an unpaired, two-tailed t-test. Three bTMB thresholds were utilized in this context: 10 mut/Mb, based on the pan-cancer approval of pembrolizumab in metastatic solid tumors26; 20.1 mut/Mb, derived from assay data indicating this value as the 80th percentile of reported TMB values in patients with CRC27; and 28 mut/Mb, based on randomized phase II data demonstrating that blood TMB above this value was predictive of benefit from durvalumab with tremelimumab in microsatellite stable mCRC28.

Subanalysis of PIK3CA exon 9 and 20 mutations

A subanalysis assessed samples from July 2021–Jun 2022 (7773 samples). This cohort was analyzed manually to assess specific co-occurring patterns with exon 9 and 20 PIK3CA alterations. All synonymous mutations and variants of unknown significance were removed. The first analysis assessed frequency differences of PIK3CA alterations by exons 9 or 20 between BRAF V600E and BRAF mutations excluding V600E (“BRAF non-V600E mutations”). This analysis was also conducted on KRAS genes, specifically KRAS G12C vs. KRAS G13D vs. samples with KRAS-ND. Co-occurrence by PIK3CA exons 9, exon 20, and PIK3CA samples with exon 9 and 20 mutations were analyzed with a two-sided Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Patient and sample characteristics

ctDNA results with ≥1 alteration detected were available for 17,622 samples. Of these, 56.0% of patients were male and the median age at ctDNA testing was 63 years old (range 19–100) (Table 1). The majority of mutations were missense mutations (94.3%), with the remaining mutation classes including in-frame, truncating, and splice mutations. Mutations were found across the gene with a high frequency of genes in exons 9 and 20 (Fig. 1B). We next examined the rates of RAS (KRAS/NRAS/HRAS) mutations and found RAS co-occurrence in 67.4% of PIK3CAm samples. We examined the frequencies of co-occurring KRAS G12C and G13D based on therapies currently available and in development targeting these alterations. We assessed PIK3CAm by exon as above for KRAS G12C, KRAS G13D, and KRAS-ND groups (Fig. 5B). KRAS G12C and G13D demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of co-occurring PIK3CA exon 9 mutations (60.7% and 61.6%, respectively) as compared to KRAS-ND (44.4%, p ≤ 0.0068). There were no significant differences in PIK3CA exon 20 mutations by KRAS group. For combined exon 9 and 20 mutations, there was a significant increase in KRAS G12C vs. KRAS-ND (8.3% vs. 2.3%, p = 0.0083), but no differences between KRAS G13D and G12C nor KRAS G13D and KRAS-ND.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer is a complex disease characterized by intricate signaling interactions that are not yet fully understood. In this investigation, we analyzed circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) from over 17,000 advanced CRC samples, constituting the largest cohort to date, to elucidate the genomic landscape of PIK3CA mutations. Our findings show that PIK3CA mutations were present in 19.2% of the samples and 21.0% of patients, aligning with previous reports of prevalence rates between 10% and 30%16. Furthermore, our results corroborate existing literature regarding the distribution of PIK3CA mutations, demonstrating a predominance of mutations in the helical domain (exon 9), particularly E545K, over mutations in the kinase domain (exon 20)9,10,14,29.

While PI3K inhibitors have shown significant survival benefits in treating other cancers, such as breast and endometrial30, the use of PI3K inhibitors in CRC has not been as successful. A phase II trial of PX-866 (a pan-PI3K inhibitor) with Cetuximab in mCRC showed no improvement in PFS, objective response rate, or OS and resulted in increased toxicity31. In another study by Garrido-Laguna et. al, analyzing outcomes in patients with advanced colorectal cancer treated with PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis inhibitors in early-phase trials, only one out of 15 patients with PIK3CA mutations achieved stable disease for 6 months or more32. These findings underscore the limitations of PI3K inhibitors, particularly their lack of specificity, which contributes to high toxicity, dose-limiting side effects, and reduced clinical efficacy33,34. To address these challenges, combination therapies are suggested to enhance efficacy, prolong the duration of response, and address modest response rates33,34. Additionally, future drug development efforts aim to create next-generation PI3K inhibitors that are more selective for mutant proteins or specific isoforms, which could reduce non-specific toxicities and improve therapeutic windows33.

We also examined the differences in co-occurring features between PIK3CAm vs. PIK3CA-ND samples. Compared to PIK3CA-ND samples, PIK3CAm samples showed significantly more alterations per sample and higher rates of co-occurrence with 80 out of 82 reported genes. Notably, PIK3CAm samples had an elevated frequency of mutations in key oncogenic signaling pathways, including the Ras/MAPK and Wnt pathways. Moreover, PIK3CAm samples demonstrated a significantly higher median TMB compared to PIK3CA-ND samples (p < 0.0001), with over a quarter of PIK3CAm samples exceeding the 80th percentile (bTMB ≥ 20.1 mut/Mb). Similar findings of increased genetic alterations and mutation burden have been reported in PIK3CAm tumors10, although the causes behind such patterns remain unclear. Some researchers propose that this elevated mutation burden, despite the absence of clear prognostic significance, suggests that PIK3CAm arise as passenger mutations, likely as byproducts of underlying genomic instability35. If PIK3CAm were truly passenger mutations, however, their frequent occurrence in hotspot regions would be unlikely10. Furthermore, several studies have linked reduced survival outcomes with PIK3CAm when stratifying by exon location, suggesting that these mutations play a more active role in tumor progression8,20,36. While the full impact of PIK3CAm on tumor behavior remains under investigation, the associated genomic instability likely contributes to the resistance observed in these tumors to PI3K inhibitor monotherapy11. Thus, advancing therapeutic strategies in PIK3CAm CRC will require combinatorial approaches targeting the oncogenic pathways activated in these tumors.

The complex signaling landscape among PIK3CAm tumors may also contribute to resistance against several therapies, such as EGFR inhibitors. While KRAS mutations are canonical biomarkers of resistance to EGFR inhibitors, continued resistance in up to 65% of KRAS-wild-type patients is attributed to additional mutations affecting signaling cascades downstream of EGFR37. These include alterations in the RAS/RAF/MEK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, including PI3CKA8,14,17,20,38. A recent meta-analysis indicates that mCRC patients with PI3CKA mutations exhibit both a poorer response to anti-EGFR therapy (relative risk 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.82) and a significantly shorter median OS (35.5 vs. 55.3 months, p = 0.003) compared to wild-type patients20. PI3CKAm may enable therapeutic resistance through direct activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway or by indirectly impacting other pathways, such as the WNT/β-catenin pathway38,39. While these findings may suggest that the addition of PI3K inhibitors to EGFR therapies may potentially overcome resistance, clinical trials have yielded disappointing results. A randomized phase II trial among KRAS wild-type mCRC patients naïve to anti-EGFR therapy showed that combining PX-866, a pan-isoform PI3K inhibitor, with cetuximab led to higher toxicity without improvement of PFS or OS31. A potential explanation for these outcomes is that pan-isoform PI3K inhibitors target both cancerous and non-cancerous cells indiscriminately, resulting in toxicities that limit their therapeutic potential33. In contrast, newer, isoform-specific PI3K inhibitors show greater efficacy and more favorable toxicity profiles, making their combination with EGFR inhibitors a promising strategy33. In KRAS mutant colorectal cancers, strategies to overcome EGFR resistance must also include therapeutics to address constitutive activation of the RAS-MAPK pathway. This strategy was validated in preclinical models, where resistance to anti-EGFR antibodies in PIK3CA/KRAS double-mutant CRC cells was reversed with triple therapy targeting MEK, PI3K, and HER240. Given its potential to target the escape mechanisms driving resistance in tumors harboring both PIK3CA and KRAS mutations, triple therapy may offer a promising approach in this patient population and should be further evaluated in clinical trials.

Our results align with prior reports showing a pronounced co-occurrence of PIK3CAm and KRASm in mCRC8–10,20,29. Given the association of KRAS/PIK3CA double mutants with increased tumor aggressiveness and poorer clinical outcomes, novel therapeutic strategies targeting these alterations are urgently needed2,41–43. We observed a significant increase in KRAS G12C mutations compared to KRAS-ND among combined exon 9 and 20 PIK3CAm samples, suggesting the utility of KRAS G12C inhibitors in this subset. Clinical trials have shown that combining KRAS G12C inhibitors with EGFR inhibitors is effective in treating CRC, although secondary resistance limits their long-term use44,45. A primary mechanism of this resistance involves hyperactivation of the PI3K pathway46; however, preclinical data demonstrates that the combination of KRAS G12C inhibitors and PI3K inhibitors can reverse this resistance47–49. The strategic use of mutant-selective and non-selective inhibitors may also mitigate toxicities that would otherwise limit the full suppressive potential of targeted therapies. For example, in cancers possessing KRAS G12C mutations, combining KRAS G12C and PI3K inhibitors would inhibit the MEK/ERK and PI3K pathways in cancer cells while only suppressing the PI3K/mTOR pathway in non-cancerous cells50. Such a combination could limit tumor escape mechanisms while maintaining a favorable side effect profile, thereby maximizing the full therapeutic potential of targeted therapies50.

Immunotherapy may also play a pivotal role in combinatorial strategies in PIK3CAm tumors. Elevated TMB has been linked to enhanced immunotherapy response51, likely due to increased neoantigen presentation and subsequent immune system activation. While PD-1 inhibitors are FDA-approved for solid tumors with a tissue TMB ≥ 10 mut/Mb, corresponding blood TMB values to guide immunotherapy use in CRC remain absent in current FDA guidelines52. Although various bTMB thresholds have been proposed28,51,53, further research is needed to better understand the relationship between bTMB and immunotherapy outcomes in CRC. Moreover, research indicates that mutations in the PI3K/AKT pathway can suppress various anti-tumoral immune responses, potentially diminishing the efficacy of immunotherapy52. For example, a pre-clinical study found that PI3K/AKT pathway mutations promoted regulatory T-cell proliferation, which was mitigated by PI3K or AKT inhibitor monotherapy54. The ability of PI3K inhibitors to impede the immunotolerant properties of cancer may also sensitize tumors to immunotherapy. For example, a study showed that PIK3CAm tumor models resistant to PD-1 inhibitors became responsive when PI3K inhibitors were added to the treatment55,56. In triple-negative breast cancer models, the combination of PI3KCA and CDK4/6 inhibitors was shown to synergistically enhance antigen presentation and increase the cytotoxicity of tumor-infiltrating T-cells, leading to improved disease control57. In CRC, the combination of copanlisib (a pan-PI3K inhibitor) with immune checkpoint inhibitors is currently under investigation in a phase I/II study involving microsatellite stable CRC patients52,58,59. Future studies should continue exploring the potential of PI3K inhibitors to enhance immunotherapy effectiveness in CRC combinatorial treatment strategies.

Our study is not without limitations. The lack of comprehensive clinical annotations limits our ability to directly correlate genetic findings with clinical outcomes. Additionally, including samples from both newly diagnosed and progressive disease cases introduces variability that may complicate the interpretation of the results. The retrospective nature of the study also introduces potential selection bias, as the Guardant360 database predominantly includes patients with advanced-stage disease, which may not be representative of all CRC stages. Nonetheless, by analyzing the largest cohort of advanced CRC samples to date (N = 17,622) using ctDNA next generation sequencing, our findings reinforce existing literature and offer more comprehensive insights into the impact of PIK3CAm on the genomic landscape of advanced CRC.

Our study demonstrates that PIK3CA mutations are prevalent in 19.2% of advanced colorectal cases, often co-occurring with other oncogenic alterations and associated with a higher tumor mutational burden. PIK3CAm tumors exhibit greater genomic instability, which likely contributes to therapeutic resistance and poor clinical outcomes. Our findings underscore the need for combinatorial therapeutic strategies targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway and other oncogenic pathways activated in PIK3CAm tumors to improve treatment efficacy. Moreover, our study highlights the potential for integrating PI3K inhibitors with immunotherapy to overcome resistance mechanisms and improve patient outcomes. Further clinical trials are essential to validate these approaches and optimize treatment protocols for CRC patients with PIK3CA mutations.
