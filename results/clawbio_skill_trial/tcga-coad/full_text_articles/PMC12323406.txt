# Adagrasib in the treatment of colorectal cancer

Authors: Sara F. Haddad, Youssef Bouferraa, Kanika G. Nair

Journal: Future Oncology

PMCID: PMC12323406

PMID: 40619745

DOI: 10.1080/14796694.2025.2524311

Canonical source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12323406/

## Abstract

KRASG12C mutations occur in 3–4% of metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) cases and confer a poor prognosis. Adagrasib is an oral, small molecule that selectively and irreversibly inhibits KRASG12C. The phase I/Ib KRYSTAL-1 study established 600 mg twice daily as a recommended phase II dose. Adagrasib monotherapy showed efficacy in heavily pretreated patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC. When combined with cetuximab, an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody, efficacy was further improved with an ORR of 34% and median OS of 15.9 months, leading to the accelerated approval of the combination by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in USA in 2024, but it is not yet approved in the European Union. Adagrasib is a well-tolerated drug as monotherapy and in combination with cetuximab and represents a novel treatment for KRASG12C-mutated mCRC.

## Full article

## Introduction

Prognosis for unresectable metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) remains poor, with a 5-year relative overall survival rate of less than 20% from the time of diagnosis, despite advancements in systemic therapies [1]. A critical aspect of treatment of mCRC is its genomic landscape with mutations in key genes, such as KRAS, which are present in approximately 40% of mCRC, BRAF, particularly the BRAFV600E variant which occurs in about 5–10% of mCRC, and TP53, found in approximately 50–60% of cases [2–6]. Increasingly, molecular markers influence treatment decisions in mCRC by guiding the selection of targeted therapies and predicting treatment response. KRASG12C mutation has been found to be a clinically significant mutation in mCRC, with a prevalence of 3.2% [7,8]. This discovery offered a new targeted therapeutic option to these patients, such as sotorasib (LUMAKRAS®) and adagrasib (KRAZATI™). This review offers a thorough examination of adagrasib, covering its mechanism of action, clinical trial evidence, side effects, and other essential aspects of this treatment approach.

## Overview of the field

### Unmet needs

The treatment of local and locally advanced CRC consists of a multimodal approach that includes surgical resection and cytotoxic chemotherapy as well as radiotherapy for rectal cancer [9]. However, systemic therapies are the only therapeutic option for the majority of cases with metastatic disease [9]. These include combination chemotherapy with drugs such as fluoropyrimidines, oxaliplatin, or irinotecan, as well as targeted antibodies, like those targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) [9,10]. While these treatments have improved overall survival, several unmet needs persist, particularly in patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC, which are associated with real-world data showing worse overall survival of 16.1 months as compared to 18.3 months for KRAS non-G12C mutated mCRC and 23.2 months for KRAS and BRAF-wild type mCRC [11]. This is partly secondary to resistance to anti-EGFR therapy due to the KRAS-mediated continuous activation of the MAP kinase pathway [11,12]. The inability to directly target KRAS mutations leaves a significant gap in the effective treatment of this subset of patients.

Approximately 40% of CRC have a KRAS mutation, making it a coveted molecular target [13]. Studies have explored potential treatments targeting KRAS, including downstream inhibitors of the KRAS pathway, direct anti-KRAS small molecules, and agents that modulate the post-translational regulation of KRAS [14]. Many of these therapeutic attempts did not achieve their primary outcomes due to the high affinity of the small GTP-binding pocket of KRAS with its GTP substrate and a critical need for therapies in mCRC harboring KRAS mutations remains.

### Other products in clinic/late development

A major advancement in the development of KRAS-targeting therapy occurred in 2013, when a small pocket in the mutant KRASG12C protein was identified [15]. This led to the development of a covalent inhibitor that binds the mutated G12C form, changing its preference to GDP from GTP, and thus impairing its binding to RAF [16]. This discovery of mutation-specific inhibition constituted an important step not only toward the targeted inhibition of mutated KRAS, but also in the reduction of potential side effects of the nonselective inhibition of KRAS in normal cells and mutated cancer cells. The first direct KRASG12C inhibitor with cellular potency was ARS-853 [17]. This compound, however, suffered from poor bioavailability [17,18]. The first KRASG12C inhibitor introduced in clinical trials was sotorasib, followed by adagrasib [18,19]. Other potential inhibitors include ARS-1620, SML-8–73–1, ASP2453, as well as pan-KRAS inhibitors [20–22].

#### Sotorasib

Sotorasib is a first-in-class direct selective mutated KRASG12C inhibitor developed by Amgen [23]. In preclinical studies, sotorasib-induced regression of KRASG12C mutated tumors as a single agent or with combination with chemotherapy or other targeted therapies [24]. In a phase I trial in 2019, sotorasib was shown to be well tolerated in locally advanced and metastatic KRASG12C mutated tumors with no reported drug limiting toxicities [25]. This was followed by the phase I trial CodeBreaK 100, where dose escalation was initially performed followed by expansion in mCRC and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cohorts [26]. The objective response rates (ORR) were 7.1% and 32.2% for mCRC and NSCLC, respectively. 88.1% of NSCLC and 73.8% of mCRC patient had disease control. In the phase II CodeBreaK100 trial with 124 previously treated patients with KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC, sotorasib provided an ORR of 37.1%, a disease control rate (DCR) of 80.6% and a complete response rate (CR) of 3.2%. These findings led to the accelerated approval of sotorasib for the treatment of KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC in the USA by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and in the European Union by the European Commission [27,28]. A phase III trial of 345 patients with KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC compared second-line sotorasib versus standard of care docetaxel and showed a statistically significant difference of progression-free survival (PFS) favoring sotorasib [29].

The single-arm phase II CodeBreaK 100 trial also evaluated sotorasib in advanced KRASG12C-mutated cancers as single agent [30]. Sixty-two patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC received at least one dose of 960 mg of sotorasib. Unlike in NSCLC, sotorasib had limited activity as a single agent in mCRC, with an ORR of only 9.7%, possibly due to other mutations of pathways driving tumor growth and bypassing the inhibition of KRAS [31]. Therefore, CodeBreaK 101 evaluated sotorasib with panitumumab in 48 patients with chemotherapy-refractory KRASG12C-mutated mCRC and demonstrated improved efficacy with ORR of 30%, PFS of 5.7 months, and median OS of 15.2 months and tolerable safety profile [32].

The phase III CodeBreaK 300 trial evaluated the efficacy of sotorasib and panitumumab, an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody, versus the investigator’s choice standard of care therapy (SOC) with trifluridine – tipiracil or regorafenib in previously treated KRASG12C-mutated mCRC [33]. One hundred and sixty patients underwent 1:1:1 randomization to sotorasib 960 mg with panitumumab, sotorasib 240 mg with panitumumab, or SOC. After a median follow-up of 13.6 months, the sotorasib 960 mg with panitumumab arm had an ORR of 30.2% and PFS of 5.7 months versus SOC arm with ORR of 1.9% and PFS of 2.0 months [33,34]. In contrast, the sotorasib 240 mg with panitumumab arm had an ORR of 7.5% and PFS of 4.0 months. Median OS was not reached for the sotorasib 960 mg with panitumumab arm, as compared to 10.3 months for the SOC arm, but this was not statistically significant; median OS in the sotorasib 240 mg with panitumumab arm was 11.9 months [34]. Each of the sotorasib with panitumumab arms had one patient with a complete response. Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 35.8% of patients, with skin toxicity, hypomagnesemia, diarrhea, nausea, and anemia being most common. No treatment-related deaths were observed [33].

Sotorasib received orphan drug designation and priority review designation by the FDA in the USA KRASG12C-mutated mCRC in 2019. In combination with panitumumab IV 6 mg/kg every 14 days, sotorasib 960 mg once daily was FDA approved for the treatment of KRASG12C-mutated mCRC in January 2025 [35]. Sotorasib is not approved for the treatment of KRASG12C-mutated mCRC in the European Union.

#### Pan-KRAS inhibitors

Pan-KRAS inhibitors represent another approach to targeting KRAS-driven cancers [36]. Unlike KRASG12C-specific inhibitors, pan-KRAS inhibitors aim to target multiple KRAS mutations, including KRASG12D-, KRASG12V-, KRASG13D-, KRASG12R-, and KRASG12A, as well as KRAS wild-type-amplified cancers and cancers with acquired resistance to KRASG12C mutations. Therefore, pan-KRAS inhibitors have increased applicability across different tumor types, including mCRC. These inhibitors are still in the early stages of development, but preclinical studies have shown promising results, suggesting that they may offer a more comprehensive approach to KRAS-mutated cancers [37].

## Introduction to the compound

### Pharmacology

#### Chemistry

Adagrasib (MRTX849) is a covalent small molecule inhibitor that targets switch-II pocket (SII-P), with the molecular formula C32H35ClFN7O2 and molecular weight of 604.1 g/mol [38]. It targets the nucleophilicity of the newly acquired cysteine amino acid. It has a formal charge of 0. It forms polar bonds with G10, the ε-amino group of K16, and the NE2 nitrogen of H95 [39]. It also makes hydrophobic contact with V9, K16, Y96, Q99, I100, V103, and switch II residues T58, A59, Q61, E62, Y64, R68, and M72. Unlike sotorasib, H95, Y96, and Q99 do not form a cryptic pocket that interacts with adagrasib.

#### Pharmacokinetics

Adagrasib is orally administered and easily absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract [40]. The bioavailability of adagrasib is influenced by its physicochemical properties, such as lipophilicity, which enhances its ability to cross biological membranes. Following oral administration, peak plasma concentrations (C_max) are typically achieved within 4–6 hours. The absorption of adagrasib is relatively unaffected by food, allowing for flexibility in dosing regimens. Upon absorption, adagrasib exhibits a moderate volume of distribution (942 L), indicating its ability to distribute well into tissues, including tumor tissues. Adagrasib is highly protein-bound (~90–98%), primarily to albumin, which is typical for small-molecule inhibitors. This protein binding can influence the drug’s distribution and duration of action, as only the unbound fraction is pharmacologically active. The drug’s distribution is also influenced by its ability to penetrate the blood–brain barrier, which is crucial for treating potential metastases in the central nervous system (CNS) [41].

Adagrasib is primarily metabolized in the liver by cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, specifically CYP3A4. The metabolism of adagrasib results in several metabolites, some of which may retain partial activity. Understanding the metabolic pathway is essential for predicting drug–drug interactions, particularly with other medications that are substrates, inhibitors, or inducers of CYP3A4. Adagrasib also inhibits its own metabolism via CYP3A4, allowing other enzymes like CYP2C8, CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6 to contribute to its metabolism at steady state [42]. The drug’s half-life is 23 h and the apparent oral clearance is 37 L/h [40]. Twice daily dosing allows for continuous inhibition of KRAS. Excretion of adagrasib and its metabolites occurs predominantly through the feces, with a smaller fraction eliminated in the urine. Following a single oral dose, about 75% of the dose is recovered in feces (14% as unchanged) and 4.5% in urine (2% as unchanged). Given its primary excretion via feces, dose adjustments may be necessary, and steady-state is achieved within 8 days with approximately 6-fold accumulation.

#### Pharmacodynamics

KRAS is a small GTPase that functions as a molecular switch, cycling between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state [43]. The G12C mutation locks KRAS in a constitutively active state, driving oncogenic signaling pathways such as the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways and promoting tumor growth and survival. Adagrasib works by binding irreversibly and covalently to a cysteine residue in the KRASG12C-mutated protein, a common driver mutation in these cancers. By binding to this mutant cysteine at position 12, adagrasib locks the KRASG12C protein in its inactive GDP-bound state thus preventing the KRAS protein from transitioning to its active GTP-bound state, which is necessary for its role in driving oncogenic signaling. Thus, the inactive KRASG12C protein cannot propagate signals to downstream effectors in the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways. Adagrasib selectively targets the mutant KRASG12C protein which prevents the activation of downstream signaling pathways that promote tumor growth and survival without affecting the normal wild-type KRAS. This specificity helps to minimize off-target effects and potential toxicity to normal cells [39,40]. The covalent nature of adagrasib’s binding to KRASG12C ensures prolonged inhibition of the mutant protein, even after the drug is cleared from the bloodstream. This sustained target engagement is crucial for maintaining therapeutic efficacy over time. The pharmacodynamic effects of adagrasib can be monitored through biomarkers such as phosphorylated ERK (pERK) levels, which are downstream of KRAS signaling. Reductions in pERK levels in tumor biopsies from patients treated with adagrasib provide evidence of effective target inhibition. Additionally, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays can be used to assess KRASG12C mutation levels in patients, serving as a pharmacodynamic marker of drug efficacy. The dose–response relationship for adagrasib has been studied extensively in preclinical models and early-phase clinical trials. While higher doses correlate with greater KRASG12C inhibition and antitumor activity, they also increase the likelihood of TRAEs. The optimal dose of adagrasib balances maximum therapeutic effect with a tolerable safety profile, leading to the establishment of recommended doses for clinical use. ### Resistance mechanisms

As with most targeted therapies, resistance to adagrasib can develop over time. Resistance mechanisms are multifaceted, involving both tumor cell-intrinsic adaptations and bypass signaling pathways. A major reason for intrinsic resistance is feedback reactivation of upstream receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), especially the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). KRAS inhibition in CRC cells relieves negative feedback on EGFR and other RTKs, causing rebound activation of RAS/MAPK signaling through wild-type RAS isoforms. Essentially, the cancer cell finds a “detour” around the blocked KRASG12C. This adaptive RTK signaling often occurs via EGFR – SOS1–SHP2 and maintains a pool of active GTP-bound RAS, thus blunting the inhibitor’s effect [48]. Combination therapy with EGFR inhibitors (e.g., cetuximab or panitumumab) has proven highly effective at overcoming this intrinsic resistance in CRC [43].

Mechanisms of resistance may include secondary mutations in KRAS that prevent drug binding, activation of alternative signaling pathways, or adaptive responses in the tumor microenvironment [49]. Awad et al. performed histologic and genomic analysis on pre- and post-resistant tumor samples from a total of 38 patients with KRASG12C-mutated cancers that progressed on Adagrasib monotherapy. Putative mechanisms of resistance were noted in 17 patients, with 18% of patients having multiple mechanisms of resistance. Some tumor samples had acquired KRAS alterations including G13D, R68S, G12D/R/V/W, Q61H, Y96C, H95D/R/Q or a high-level of amplification of the G12C allele. Some of these mutations directly affected the drug-binding pocket, while others targeted oncogenic hotspots that lead to increased GDP-GTP exchange or decreased GTP hydrolysis. In addition, acquired activating mutations in NRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1, RET, as well as MET amplifications, were noted. These multiple pathway alterations support the current rationale of clinical trials combining KRASG12C inhibitors with inhibitors of RTKs or SHP2 (PTPN11) to target these adaptive resistance mechanisms (NCT04330664 and NCT04185883). Other genetic alterations included loss of function mutations in PTEN and NF1, as well as oncogenic fusions in ALK, RET, FGFR3, RAF1, and BRAF. Understanding these resistance mechanisms is critical for developing combination therapies or next-generation inhibitors with different modes of binding all allelic specificities that can overcome resistance and prolong the efficacy of treatment. Large scale clinical trials with KRASG12C inhibitors and sequential NGS testing using repeat tumor biopsies or ctDNA at progression to better understand the genomic changes that precede the development of resistance are needed [50]. ## Clinical efficacy

### Adagrasib

#### KRYSTAL-1 phase I/Ib

KRYSTAL-1 was a phase I/Ib study aimed to evaluate the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and clinical activity of adagrasib in patients with advanced KRASG12C-mutated solid tumors and determine the biologically relevant doses and the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) [44]. The dose-escalation phase used an accelerated titration (AT) design, followed by a modified toxicity probability interval (mTPI) design once a certain toxicity level was reached. Twenty-five patients aged ≥18 with unresectable or metastatic KRASG12C-mutated tumors and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1 were enrolled. Of these, 18 patients had NSCLC, 4 had CRC, 2 had mucinous appendiceal carcinoma, and 1 had duodenal adenocarcinoma.

The initial dosing regimen was based on dog toxicology studies, with a starting dose of 150 mg once daily. Further doses in AT design included 300 mg once daily, 600 mg once daily, 1,200 mg once daily, or 600 mg twice daily. Key endpoints included adverse events, pharmacokinetics data, and disease response, evaluated using RECIST 1.1 criteria.

Patients underwent regular disease assessments and imaging, while statistical analyses focused on ORR, PFS, and OS. The median follow-up was 22.8 months. No dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed at doses up to 600 mg once daily, but at 1,200 mg once daily, one patient experienced a DLT due to emesis, likely due to the pill burden of taking 12 pills at once. As a result, the recommended phase II dose (RP2D) was established at 600 mg twice daily. This dose was then evaluated in the expansion cohorts during phases 1b and II. Pharmacokinetic analysis showed adequate drug exposure at this dose, with no further accumulation after day 8. Thus, the recommended phase II dose of 600 mg twice daily was selected based on the maximum tolerated dose and pharmacokinetic data from phase I, showing optimal drug exposure with manageable safety. TRAEs occurred in 92% of patients, with the most common being nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and fatigue. One patient experienced a treatment-related fatal pneumonitis. This is further discussed in section 6 safety and tolerability.

At the RP2D, 53.3% of patients with NSCLC had a confirmed response, with a median duration of response (DOR) of 16.4 months and PFS of 11.1 months. The median OS for NSCLC in the subsequent phase II cohort was 12.6 months [51]. PFS for the two patients with mCRC was 3.3 and 5.5 months, with OS of 9.5 months and 10.5 months [44]. A confirmed partial response (PR) was observed in one of the two patients with KRASG12C-mutated CRC at the 600 mg twice daily dose, with a DOR of 4.2 months. Overall, adagrasib demonstrated promising activity in KRASG12C-mutated solid tumors with manageable safety at the RP2D.

#### KRYSTAL-1 phase II

The open-label, nonrandomized phase II cohort of KRYSTAL-1 evaluated the safety and efficacy of adagrasib 600 mg twice daily as monotherapy or in combination with anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody cetuximab in patients with advanced CRC harboring KRASG12C mutations [45].

The study’s primary endpoints included ORR in the monotherapy group and safety in the combination group. Secondary endpoints were DOR, PFS, OS, and safety. The trial excluded patients with untreated or active brain metastases. Safety assessments monitored TRAEs, graded according to National Cancer Institute criteria, and disease progression was evaluated with CT or MRI imaging every 6 weeks. Patients with tumor response had a confirmatory assessment at least 4 weeks after the initial response. Detailed genomic and subgroup analyses were also conducted, along with exploratory assessments of circulating tumor ctDNA kinetics and acquired genomic alterations. Subgroup analyses explored treatment efficacy based on factors such as the number of prior systemic therapies, metastatic sites, and additional genomic alterations, including mutations in TP53, PIK3CA. Statistical analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier methods, with predefined response thresholds to assess treatment efficacy.

At a median follow-up of 16.1 months, 42 patients received adagrasib monotherapy, and results showed an ORR of 19% with a disease control rate of 73.8%, PFS of 5.6 months, and OS of 12.2 months. Median DOR was 15.2 months by blind independent central review and 6.7 by investigator review. No patients had a complete response. Adagrasib demonstrated efficacy across most subgroups, including those with CNS metastases. The safety profile revealed grade 3 or higher TRAEs in 31% of patients, leading to dose reductions in 28.6% of patients and dose interruption in 35.7% of patients. Only one patient discontinued treatment due to TRAE of acute kidney injury. Nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, and vomiting were the most common TRAEs. Grade 1–2 increase in blood creatinine occurred in 26.2% of patients. There were no grade 5 events.

### Adagrasib and cetuximab

#### KRYSTAL-1 interim analysis

The results of adagrasib in combination with cetuximab cohort in patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC from the KRYSTAL-1 phase II study demonstrated enhanced response rates compared to adagrasib monotherapy [45]. Adagrasib was administered orally at a dose of 600 mg twice daily, and cetuximab was administered intravenously once weekly with a loading dose of 400 mg/m2 followed by weekly 250 mg/m2 or 500 mg/m2 once every 2 weeks. Patients were heavily pretreated with a median of three lines of therapy. Primary and secondary endpoints, clinical assessments, and statistical methods were the same as in the adagrasib monotherapy arm and noted above.

Thirty-two patients received adagrasib in combination with cetuximab. The study demonstrated enhanced efficacy compared to adagrasib monotherapy, with improved ORR of 46% and PFS of 6.9 months. The combination was well-tolerated, providing a rationale for further investigation and solidifying adagrasib’s potential as a targeted therapy in this difficult-to-treat population.

Grade 3/4 TRAEs occurred in only 6% of combination therapy patients. No grade 5 TRAEs were observed, and no patients discontinued adagrasib due to TRAEs. Exploratory ctDNA analysis showed that 55% of monotherapy patients and 88% of combination therapy patients had at least 95% clearance of the KRASG12C allele by cycle 2.

#### KRYSTALKRYSTAL-1 updated results

An updated analysis for the phase I and II KRYSTAL-1 mCRC cohorts of adagrasib and cetuximab in 94 patients was published in Cancer Discovery in 2024 with reported data as of June 2023, reinforcing the findings from the interim analysis [52]. The blinded independent central review (BICR) reported an ORR of 34.0% (95% CI, 24.6–44.5), DCR of 85.1% (95% CI, 76.3–91.6), median duration of response of 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.2–7.6), median PFS of 6.9 months, and median OS of 15.9 months (95% CI, 11.8–18.8). In comparison, the median OS for the adagrasib monotherapy cohort was 12.2 months.

A total of 36 patients (38.3%) received additional anticancer therapy. Of 49 patients who continued treatment after disease progression, post-progression scans were available for 34. Among these, 33 patients remained on adagrasib plus cetuximab, and 1 continued adagrasib alone. Tumor control was sustained in many cases, with 18 of 33 patients (54.5%) who continued the combination therapy showing tumor shrinkage at their first post-progression scan, and tumor reduction also observed in the patient continuing adagrasib monotherapy.

No new safety signals were seen in the updated results. Eight patients discontinued cetuximab due to toxicity and six of these patients continued treatment with adagrasib as a single agent without additional toxicities.

In the study, KRASG12C mutations were detected in tumor tissue for all 94 patients, and baseline plasma ctDNA was available for 83 patients. The concordance of KRASG12C detection in both tissue and ctDNA was 83%. Patients with detectable ctDNA had a higher ORR of 39% compared to 14% in those without. In the phase I patients, a 90% clearance of ctDNA at 8 weeks was associated with a 67% response rate, showing ctDNA clearance as a potential marker for response to therapy. To explore mechanisms of acquired clinical resistance, genomic alterations in plasma ctDNA were analyzed at baseline and end of therapy (EOT) in 34 patients. A total of 74% (25 patients) had at least one acquired pathogenic alteration, with 116 alterations identified overall. Many patients had multiple alterations, including RTK amplifications/fusions (35%), secondary KRAS mutations (41%), KRAS amplification (29%), and mutations/fusions in other MAPK pathway genes (38%), highlighting the complex resistance landscape in these patients [45].

These results indicate that the combination of adagrasib and cetuximab offers a promising therapeutic option for heavily pretreated patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC, providing substantial clinical benefits with a manageable safety profile.

#### KRYSTAL-10

KRYSTAL-10 is an ongoing phase III study in which patients with KRASG12C-mutated mCRC are randomly assigned 1:1 to receive adagrasib plus cetuximab in the second-line setting versus chemotherapy (either FOLFOX or FOLFIRI in combination with an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody) [53]. The KRASG12C mutation must be detected in tissue. Patients must have received fluoropyrimidine containing doublet chemotherapy in the first-line setting. The primary endpoints are PFS and OS. Preliminary results are eagerly awaited and anticipated to be available in 2026. ### Adagrasib vs sotorasib

We currently do not have clinical trials or real-world data comparing the efficacy of adagrasib to sotorasib or other KRASG12C inhibitors. While cross-trial comparison is not scientifically feasible, it is important to highlight some of the similarities and differences between the two drugs and the corresponding trials that lead to their approvals (Table 3). In both the KRYSTAL-I phase II trial and CodeBreaK 300 trial, the patient population was advanced CRC with similar median age, race, and sex distributions, with around 50% being males and around 80% having a white race [44,52]. Patients in KRYSTAL-1 received a median of two prior lines of therapy vs a median of three prior lines in CodeBreaK 300 [33,51]. Nearly all patients had received prior fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan in both trials and there was only one patient with MSI-high status in each trial. CODEBREAK 300 included 53 patients in each arm, compared to 44 and 32 patients in adagrasib monotherapy and adagrasib and cetuximab in KRYSTAL-1 [33,51]. Pharmacokinetic differences between adagrasib and sotorasib, such as half-life and CNS penetration, influence outcomes. Both drugs selectively and irreversibly inhibit KRASG12C. Adagrasib has a longer half-life of approximately 23 h and reaches a steady state within 8 days, versus a half-life of 5 h of sotorasib and time to steady state within 22 days [40,69]. As a result, adagrasib achieves sustained inhibition of KRASG12C more quickly. Adagrasib is dosed twice daily and sotorasib is once daily; both are oral medications and administered continuously. Adagrasib also has demonstrated CNS penetration in preclinical models and clinical data for NSCLC [73,74]. There is limited data on CNS penetration with sotorasib [45,75]. However, it is important to note that both trials excluded patients with active CNS metastases.

### Real-world evidence

Currently, there are limited real-world data available about the outcomes of adagrasib in patients with KRASG12C-mutated CRC. While there are some concerns from cross-trial comparisons that adagrasib is less effective in CRC as compared to NSCLC due to the resistance mechanisms mentioned above, upcoming real-world clinical data may provide insights into its effectiveness, as a monotherapy or combination therapy, in the real-world patient populations, which can be more diverse than clinical trials [76].

## Safety and tolerability

Adagrasib has been generally well-tolerated in clinical trials, with a manageable safety profile. Common TRAEs include gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue, and liver enzyme elevations, which are consistent with those observed with other targeted therapies [45,52].

No new safety concerns were identified in the expanded dataset [52]. The most common TRAEs with adagrasib plus cetuximab were nausea (60.6%), vomiting (51.6%), diarrhea (48.9%), and dermatitis acneiform (47.9%). Grade 3–4 TRAEs occurred in 27.7% of patients, with no grade 5 events. Dose reductions were needed for adagrasib in 29.8% of patients and cetuximab in 6.4%. Eight patients (8.5%) discontinued cetuximab due to TRAEs, but six continued adagrasib monotherapy. No patients discontinued adagrasib due to TRAEs.

Liver test abnormalities were commonly observed in the prelicensure clinical trials of adagrasib in patients with solid tumors harboring KRASG12C mutations, though they were typically mild and self-limiting [53,76]. ALT elevations occurred in 28% to 46% of patients receiving adagrasib, with elevations exceeding five times the upper limit of normal (ULN) in 5% to 7%. Among the approximately 366 patients enrolled in these trials, 8% discontinued adagrasib early due to increased AST or ALT levels. A small subset of patients (0.5%) experienced clinically significant hepatotoxicity, necessitating discontinuation of the drug. Liver test abnormalities had a median onset of 3 weeks after starting treatment. Although serum aminotransferase elevations occasionally reached high levels (5 to 20 times ULN), there were no associated increases in serum bilirubin, and no cases of clinically apparent liver injury with jaundice were reported. The adagrasib product label advises routine liver function monitoring prior to treatment, at three-week intervals during the first 3 months, and as clinically warranted thereafter.

In retrospective pharmacovigilance studies including adagrasib and sotorasib, the most common reported TRAEs were gastrointestinal effects (nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea), followed by hepatobiliary derangements (hepatotoxicity, elevation in liver enzymes), as well as fatigue, myalgia, dehydration, decreased appetite, peripheral edema, acute kidney injuries, pneumonitis, and pleural effusions [76,77].

Adagrasib is also known to prolong the QTc and avoiding concomitant use of other QTc-prolonging medications is recommended [70]. Grade 1–2 QTc prolongation was seen at a rate of 25% in the KRYSTAL-1 phase I/Ib study; there were no grade 3–4 events [44]. Less commonly, interstitial lung disease and pneumonitis have been reported, and it is important to note that one grade 5 TRAE of interstitial pneumonitis occurred in a patient with underlying pneumonitis associated with prior radiation and systemic therapy the KRYSTAL-1 NSCLC monotherapy cohort. The pneumonitis was presumed to be interstitial and potentially exacerbated by adagrasib [44]. No cases of pneumonitis were reported in the KRYSTAL-1 mCRC cohorts.

## Regulatory affairs

In December 2022, adagrasib was granted its first approval in the USA by the FDA for treating adults with KRASG12C-mutated locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who have received at least one prior systemic therapy [70].

Due to the phase II results of KRYSTAL-1, the combination of adagrasib and cetuximab received accelerated approval from the FDA for use in adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic KRASG12C-mutated CRC who have previously been treated with fluoropyrimidine-, oxaliplatin-, and irinotecan-based chemotherapy on 21 June 2024 [72].

The European Commission has granted conditional marketing authorization for adagrasib for the targeted treatment of KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC upon disease progression with a least one line of systemic therapy [78]. However, adagrasib either as monotherapy or in combination with cetuximab has not been granted approval yet for KRASG12C-mutated CRC.

## Conclusion

Adagrasib represents a significant advancement in the treatment of KRASG12C-mutated colorectal cancer. Adagrasib’s pharmacokinetic profile supports its use as an oral, twice-daily therapy with effective tissue penetration and sustained target inhibition. Its pharmacodynamic properties, characterized by selective and irreversible inhibition of KRASG12C, result in significant antitumor activity in KRASG12C-mutated cancers, including mCRC. When combined with anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody cetuximab in KRASG12C-mutated mCRC, the efficacy of adagrasib is further improved. Its development addresses a critical unmet need in oncology, offering hope to patients with a historically poor prognosis. While further research is necessary to fully understand its long-term efficacy and safety, particularly in combination with other therapies, the data to date are encouraging.

Looking ahead, adagrasib’s role in combination strategies, such as with immunotherapies or SHP2 inhibitors, will further personalize treatment for KRASG12C-mutated cancers. The KRYSTAL-7 trial is evaluating adagrasib in combination with pembrolizumab as first-line treatment in patients with advanced KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC, with preliminary results indicating that this combination is effective and has a favorable safety profile [56]. In preclinical studies, combining adagrasib with SHP2 inhibitors enhances antitumor activity to overcome resistance mechanisms. The KRYSTAL-2 trial aims to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of the combination of adagrasib with TNO155, a selective SHP2 inhibitor, in patients with advanced solid tumors harboring a KRASG12C mutation [59].
