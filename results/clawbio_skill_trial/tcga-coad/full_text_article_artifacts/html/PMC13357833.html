Molecular heterogeneity of ERBB2-altered cancers: A pan-cancer landscape across US and Japanese cohorts

Hiroaki Ikushima, Hideaki Motomura, Kousuke Watanabe, Aya Shinozaki-Ushiku, Katsutoshi Oda, Hidenori Kage
NPJ Precision Oncology

Abstract

Therapies targeting ERBB2 (HER2) are rapidly expanding toward tissue-agnostic indications. However, the diversity of ERBB2-altered tumors across different cancer types and populations remains poorly characterized. We performed a comprehensive pan-cancer molecular analysis of ERBB2-altered tumors using two large clinical genomic datasets: AACR Project GENIE (n = 210,640) and the nationwide Japanese comprehensive genomic profiling database (C-CAT; n = 80,600). Across all cancer types, the prevalence of ERBB2 mutations was 1.4% in GENIE and 2.2% in C-CAT, whereas ERBB2 amplification occurred in 2.1% and 6.5% of tumors, respectively. Kinase domain mutations such as L755S in breast cancer and Y772_A775dup in lung cancer were enriched in a cancer type-specific manner, while R678Q in the juxtamembrane domain was common in colorectal and gastroesophageal cancers. ERBB2 compound mutations were rare (<1% in both cohorts) and did not significantly impact overall survival in the cohort treated predominantly before the advent of novel HER2-selective targeted therapies. ERBB2 mutations and amplifications also displayed divergent co-mutational landscapes: mutations frequently co-occurred with ARID1A mutations, whereas amplifications were enriched for TP53 mutations and CCNE1 amplification. In colorectal cancer, ERBB2 mutations were associated with higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) and frequent KRAS co-mutations, while in lung adenocarcinoma, ERBB2 amplifications exhibited higher TMB and a heavier co-mutation burden. A survival analysis revealed that specific co-occurring alterations, including MAP2K1 mutations in colorectal cancer and CCNE1 amplification in breast cancer, were associated with distinct prognostic outcomes among ERBB2-amplified cases. Our findings demonstrate that “ERBB2-altered cancer” is not a homogenous entity. Optimizing precision oncology for ERBB2 requires integrating histological context, specific alteration types, and co-mutation profiles to guide therapeutic selection and overcome resistance.

Introduction

The ERBB2 gene, which encodes the transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2), is a critical proto-oncogene implicated in the progression of diverse human malignancies1,2. HER2 is unique among the ErbB family in that it is an orphan receptor with no known high-affinity ligand. Unlike EGFR (ERBB1), which typically undergoes ligand-induced homodimerization, HER2 functions primarily as the preferred heterodimerization partner for other family members3. The constitutive activation of HER2 signaling serves as a key oncogenic driver, and this is triggered by several distinct classes of genomic ERBB2 alterations, including gene amplification, in-frame insertion/deletion, and missense mutations. In-frame insertions/deletions are well-characterized within exon 20, which encodes part of the intracellular kinase domain4. Missense mutations can also confer constitutive activation through multiple distinct mechanisms. These include not only direct alterations to the intracellular kinase domain but also mutations in the transmembrane and juxtamembrane domains that stabilize the formation of active receptor dimers5,6. Furthermore, alterations in the extracellular furin-like cysteine-rich region have been reported to promote dimerization, contributing to the complex landscape of oncogenic ERBB2 modifications7,8.

In recent years, a growing repertoire of HER2-directed therapies has transformed the clinical landscape. Small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as lapatinib, neratinib, tucatinib, zongertinib, and sevabertinib target the intracellular kinase domain, while monoclonal antibodies, including trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and zanidatamab, and their derivative antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) bind the extracellular region to inhibit receptor activation or deliver cytotoxic payloads9. Given the broad involvement of HER2 signaling across tumor types, both TKIs and ADCs are being actively evaluated in a tissue-agnostic manner10,11. The US Food and Drug Administration recently granted accelerated approval to trastuzumab deruxtecan for patients with unresectable or metastatic solid tumors overexpressing HER2 (immunohistochemistry 3+)12. However, the prevalence and spectrum of ERBB2 alterations vary substantially by cancer type, and emerging evidence indicates that the therapeutic response differs markedly according to the specific mutation or structural class of the alteration, even within the same histology13,14. To advance the development of tissue-agnostic HER2-targeted therapies and to refine treatment stratification, a detailed understanding of the distribution and molecular context of ERBB2 alterations across cancer types is essential. While previous studies have mapped the broad pan-cancer landscape and hotspot distributions of ERBB2 alterations, detailed side-by-side comparisons of the clinicogenomic features between ERBB2 mutations and amplifications across diverse real-world populations remain limited. Here, we integrate two massive clinicogenomic resources: the AACR Project GENIE from the United States15 and the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C-CAT) from Japan16. By leveraging these independent cohorts, we aimed to systematically compare the distinct co-mutational profiles, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and survival trajectories associated with ERBB2 mutations versus amplifications, thereby providing deeper insights into the biological heterogeneity of HER2-driven cancers.

Results

Prevalence of ERBB2 mutations and amplifications across cancer types in GENIE and C-CAT cohorts

We first analyzed the frequencies of ERBB2 missense mutations and in-frame insertions/deletions (in-frame mutations) across cancer types, defined by OncoTree Level 117, in the GENIE (n = 210,640) and C-CAT (n = 80,600) cohorts. The overall mutation frequency was 1.4% in GENIE and 2.2% in C-CAT (Fig. 1A, Table S1). The distribution trends across cancer types were largely similar between the cohorts. Organs with a high prevalence of these mutations included the ampulla of Vater (5.8% in GENIE and 8.4% in C-CAT), biliary tract (2.1% and 4.2%), bladder (8.6% and 6.9%), bowel (2.4% and 2.3%), breast (2.6% and 3.3%), cervix (5.3% and 4.2%), lung (1.9% and 5.0%), skin (1.0% and 3.9%), stomach/esophagus (2.6% and 2.8%), and uterus (2.3% and 1.5%). While missense mutations constituted the majority of mutations overall, lung cancer was a notable exception, in which in-frame mutations were more frequent than missense mutations (GENIE: missense 0.65%, in-frame 1.2%; C-CAT: missense 1.3%, in-frame 3.7%).Fig. 1Pan-cancer prevalence of ERBB2 alterations in the GENIE and C-CAT cohorts.A Frequency of ERBB2 missense mutations and in-frame short insertions/deletions across different cancer types (OncoTree Level 1) in the GENIE (left) and C-CAT (right) cohorts. We next evaluated ERBB2 amplification using the same OncoTree framework. The overall amplification frequency was 2.1% in GENIE and 6.5% in C-CAT (Fig. 1B, Table S1). Organs with a high prevalence of ERBB2 amplification included the ampulla of Vater (2.8% in GENIE and 10.9% in C-CAT), biliary tract (3.4% and 9.8%), bladder (5.0% and 11.2%), breast (10.4% and 11.1%), head and neck (2.2% and 8.4%), lung (0.9% and 8.3%), ovary (2.2% and 8.1%), peripheral nervous system (0.7% and 8.5%), stomach/esophagus (10.4% and 13.9%), and uterus (3.4% and 8.6%). Consistent with these cohorts, similar organ-specific trends of ERBB2 mutations and amplifications were observed in the TCGA PanCancer Atlas and MSK-IMPACT validation cohorts (Fig. S1).

Although ERBB2 amplification appeared more common in the C-CAT cohort than the GENIE cohort, this discrepancy must be interpreted with extreme caution, as it is heavily confounded by inherent technical variations in sequencing panels and copy-number calling pipelines between the two databases. Indeed, comparison of ERBB2 alteration frequencies by sequencing panel demonstrated minimal panel-level differences for missense and in-frame mutations, whereas amplification frequencies varied substantially across different platforms (Fig. S2A). This platform-dependent variability persisted even when analyses were limited to specific cancer types to account for differences in cohort composition (Fig. S2B). Because there is no overlap in the specific panels utilized between the two cohorts, direct normalization or statistical cross-population comparisons of amplification frequencies are precluded. Therefore, we consider the higher ERBB2 amplification frequency in C-CAT to be primarily driven by technical heterogeneity rather than genuine biological or ethnic variation. To avoid overinterpreting platform-related bias, our subsequent analyses leverage these two independent cohorts not to claim cross-population differences, but rather to confirm the universal reproducibility of the distinct clinicogenomic features between ERBB2-mutated and ERBB2-amplified tumors.

We also examined the co-occurrence of ERBB2 mutations and amplification (Fig. S3A). This co-occurrence was most frequently observed in the ampulla of Vater (0.8% in GENIE and 2.5% in C-CAT), bladder (0.9% and 1.5%), and lung (0.2% and 1.6%) cancers. Furthermore, statistical analysis confirmed that ERBB2 mutations and amplifications significantly co-occur across the overall pan-cancer cohort and multiple major organs (Table S2). Specifically in lung cancer, 9.3% (GENIE) and 32.2% (C-CAT) of ERBB2-mutated cases harbored concurrent amplification (Fig. S3B), while 18.4% (GENIE) and 19.3% (C-CAT) of ERBB2-amplified cases harbored concurrent mutations (Fig. S3C).

Next, we compared ERBB2 mutations and amplification frequencies across specific histological subtypes for selected organs with sufficient sample sizes (Fig. 1C). In bladder cancer, urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) showed higher frequencies of both mutations and amplification relative to other subtypes. In colorectal cancer, alterations were observed not only in colorectal adenocarcinoma (COADREAD) but also in anal squamous cell carcinoma (ANSC). In breast cancer, amplification was more common in invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), whereas mutations were more frequent in invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) in both cohorts. In lung cancer, adenocarcinoma (LUAD) had the highest frequency of both mutations and amplification, though amplification was also observed in squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). In gastroesophageal cancers, alterations were primarily found in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), esophageal adenocarcinoma (ESCA), and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (GEJ). In uterine cancers, amplification was more common in serous carcinoma (USC) and clear cell carcinoma (UCCC) than in endometrioid carcinoma (UEC). Subtype-specific frequencies in biliary tract, cervical, and ovarian cancers are shown in the Supplementary Fig. S4.

We next tested whether ERBB2 alterations were associated with age or sex in bladder, colorectal, breast, lung, gastric/esophageal, and uterine cancers (tumor types with substantial numbers of ERBB2-altered cases in both cohorts). Across GENIE and C-CAT cohorts, patients with ERBB2 amplification in gastroesophageal and uterine cancers were significantly older than those without the alteration (Fig. S5A). In the GENIE cohort, patients with ERBB2 amplification in breast cancer were significantly younger. Regarding sex differences, ERBB2 mutations were more common in women with lung cancer in both cohorts, whereas ERBB2 amplification was more common in men with gastroesophageal cancers (Fig. S5B).

Organ-specific mutational profiles and hotspots of ERBB2 mutations

We next characterized the organ-specific mutational profiles of ERBB2 and compared hotspots across cancer types in both the GENIE and C-CAT cohorts (Fig. 2). In breast cancer, mutations predominantly clustered within the intracellular kinase domain, with L755S in exon 19 being the most frequent, followed by exon 20 substitutions such as V777L/M. Lung cancers also showed a high frequency of kinase domain alterations, with exon 20 in-frame insertions such as Y772_A775dup representing the predominant hotspot in both cohorts. In uterine cancer, while alterations were also enriched in the intracellular kinase domain, the most frequent mutation was V842I in exon 21. In colorectal and gastroesophageal cancers, the R678Q mutation in the juxtamembrane domain was frequently observed. In both the GENIE and C-CAT cohorts, the S310F/Y mutation, located in the extracellular furin-like cysteine-rich region, was the most prevalent alteration in bladder, biliary tract, and cervical cancers (Fig. 2, Fig. S6). Prevalence and patterns of ERBB2 compound mutations across cancer types

Compound mutations, defined as the presence of two or more concurrent mutations within the same gene, are well described in EGFR18,19. However, ERBB2 compound mutations are rare, and their prevalence and specific combinational patterns remain poorly understood. We therefore evaluated ERBB2 compound mutations across cancer types in both the GENIE and C-CAT cohorts, focusing on bladder, bowel, breast, lung, gastroesophageal, and uterine cancers. Our analysis revealed that the frequency of ERBB2 compound mutations was extremely low (<1%) in both cohorts across all analyzed cancer types (Fig. 3A). Given this rarity, and considering our prior finding that the mutational profiles for these six cancer types were similar between GENIE and C-CAT, we pooled the data from both cohorts to perform a more robust characterization. Across all cancer types, most combinations involved mutations within the intracellular kinase domain paired with another mutation in the intracellular kinase domain, the transmembrane/juxtamembrane domains, or the furin-like cysteine-rich region (Fig. 3B). In EGFR mutation-positive cancers, compound mutations are reported to confer reduced sensitivity to EGFR TKIs, resulting in a poorer prognosis20–22. In contrast, the prognostic significance of ERBB2 compound mutations remains largely unknown due to the limited number of reported cases. We therefore performed a pan-cancer survival analysis to maximize statistical power. In our exploratory analysis, we observed no statistically significant difference in overall survival between patients harboring compound mutations and those with simple (single) ERBB2 mutations (Fig. 3C). However, given the limited number of events in the compound mutation cohort, this finding should be considered preliminary and interpreted with extreme caution. Furthermore, it is important to contextualize these results by noting that the survival data were predominantly derived from periods prior to the clinical availability of next-generation, HER2-selective TKIs, such as zongertinib and sevabertinib.

Landscape of co-occurring alterations with ERBB2 mutations and amplifications

Co-occurring genomic alterations outside the primary target gene are known to influence both sensitivity and acquired resistance to targeted therapies23,24. We therefore directly compared the co-mutation profiles of ERBB2-mutated tumors with those of ERBB2-amplified tumors to elucidate the distinct biological backgrounds of these two alteration types. Because the sensitivity for detecting co-occurring alterations may differ across sequencing panels, analyses were performed separately for the GENIE and C-CAT cohorts. The CDK12 gene, which resides in close proximity to ERBB2 (17q12) and is frequently co-amplified in ERBB2-amplified tumors due to regional copy-number gains, was excluded from the comparison of co-mutational patterns between ERBB2-mutated tumors and ERBB2-amplified tumors. In COADREAD, ERBB2-mutated tumors showed higher frequencies of KRAS and ARID1A mutations in both cohorts, whereas ERBB2-amplified tumors exhibited a higher prevalence of TP53 mutations (Fig. 4A). In IDC, ERBB2-mutated tumors more frequently harbored MAP3K1 mutations in comparison to ERBB2-amplified tumors (Fig. 4B). In LUAD, ERBB2-amplified tumors showed enrichment for multiple co-occurring alterations including TP53, EGFR, MET, SMARCA4, and CCNE1 in comparison to ERBB2-mutated tumors in both cohorts (Fig. 5A). In STAD, ERBB2-mutated tumors showed a high frequency of ARID1A mutations, whereas ERBB2-amplified tumors were associated with higher frequencies of TP53 and CCNE1 alterations (Fig. 5B). Prognostic analysis of ERBB2 mutations versus amplifications and co-mutation stratification

We compared overall survival between patients with ERBB2-mutated and ERBB2-amplified tumors in the C-CAT cohort (Fig. 7A). Survival outcomes were comparable between the two groups in COADREAD, IDC, and STAD. Consistent results were observed in a subgroup analysis restricted to patients treated with trastuzumab deruxtecan (Fig. S8). Next, we focused on the ERBB2-amplified population to identify molecular determinants of the prognosis. While the development of therapies targeting ERBB2 amplification has benefited many patients, primary resistance and the acquisition of secondary resistance remain significant clinical challenges. To explore potential molecular mechanisms contributing to these outcomes, we analyzed whether co-occurring alterations could stratify prognosis within the ERBB2-amplified population in the C-CAT cohort and identified alterations that were significantly associated with poor prognosis in each cancer type (Fig. 7B). These included MAP2K1 mutations in COADREAD, CCNE1 amplification in IDC, MDM2 amplification in LUAD, and ARID1A mutations in STAD.

Discussion

This study integrates two of the largest clinicogenomic datasets from the United States (GENIE) and Japan (C-CAT) to directly compare the clinical and molecular landscapes of ERBB2 mutations versus amplifications. As the therapeutic paradigm shifts towards tissue-agnostic strategies driven by the advent of novel ADCs and TKIs, understanding the precise clinicopathological and molecular diversity underlying different ERBB2 alteration types has become increasingly critical. By systematically analyzing more than 290,000 tumors across diverse cancer types, we expand upon known prevalence and organ-specific distributions by providing a side-by-side comparison of their distinct genomic backgrounds. We demonstrated that ERBB2-mutated and ERBB2-amplified tumors exhibit substantial biological divergence, not only in clinical outcomes but also in their co-mutational profiles and TMB. Importantly, distinct co-occurring alterations were associated with the prognosis among ERBB2-amplified cancers, highlighting the biological and clinical diversity within this subgroup. Collectively, these findings provide a refined framework for understanding the molecular heterogeneity of ERBB2-altered tumors, highlighting the necessity of integrating histological context, specific alteration types, and co-mutation profiles to optimize patient stratification and therapeutic development in precision oncology.

An important observation in this study was the higher overall frequency of ERBB2 amplification in the C-CAT cohort in comparison to the GENIE cohort. While this finding may suggest potential population-level or racial differences, careful interpretation is required. Differences in panel design, copy-number calling algorithms, and analytic pipelines between the two datasets likely contribute substantially to this discrepancy, as supported by our panel-level analyses showing greater variability in amplification detection than in missense/in-frame mutation detection. Nonetheless, certain findings, such as the difference in ERBB2 mutation hotspots in ovarian cancer between the two cohorts, raise the possibility of genuine genetic or etiologic differences across populations. Distinguishing true biological variation from technical artifacts will require harmonized analytic pipelines.

Our detailed mapping of ERBB2 mutations revealed organ-specific hotspot patterns, highlighting the structural and biological diversity of HER2 activation across tumor types. Mutations within the intracellular kinase domain, including Y772_A775dup in lung cancer, result in constitutive receptor activation25. L755S mutation within the intracellular kinase domain has been reported to cause resistance to lapatinib in breast cancer patients26,27. In contrast, alterations in the extracellular furin-like cysteine-rich region, such as S310F/Y, which were common in bladder, biliary tract, and cervical cancers, stabilize receptor dimerization through hydrophobic interactions with the neighboring molecule7,8. Colorectal and gastroesophageal tumors frequently harbored R678Q mutations within the juxtamembrane domain, which enhance HER2 activity by improving the active dimer interface or stabilizing an activating conformation6. These organ-specific hotspots may have direct therapeutic implications: kinase domain mutations, particularly exon 20 insertions, may influence sensitivity to HER2-targeted TKIs28,29, while extracellular domain mutations may modulate binding affinity and efficacy of ADCs30,31. Recent results from the Beamion LUNG-1 trial provide compelling clinical validation for this domain-centric approach. The study reported differences in efficacy based on mutation location, with zongertinib achieving an objective response rate of 71% in patients harboring kinase domain mutations, compared with only 30% in those with non-kinase domain alterations28. The mutation site within ERBB2, therefore, represents a critical biological determinant that may inform the selection and sequencing of HER2-directed therapies in a tissue-agnostic context.

The clear divergence in co-mutational patterns between ERBB2 mutations and amplification underscores the fundamentally different biological processes underlying these two modes of HER2 pathway activation. ERBB2 mutations were frequently associated with alterations in chromatin remodeling genes such as ARID1A (in COADREAD, IDC, STAD), suggesting that these tumors may arise in a genomic context characterized by epigenetic dysregulation and/or genomic instability32,33. In contrast, ERBB2-amplified tumors were enriched for co-alterations in TP53 (COADREAD, LUAD, STAD) and CCNE1 (LUAD, STAD), implicating impaired DNA damage response and cell-cycle deregulation as key features of amplification-driven tumor biology34,35. The observation that COADREAD exhibited a broader spectrum of co-mutated genes in the mutation group, whereas LUAD showed more extensive co-alterations in the amplification group, further suggests that the interplay between ERBB2 alteration type and tissue-specific oncogenic processes shapes the resulting genomic landscape. This genomic context may explain the significant prognostic disparity in LUAD, where ERBB2 amplification was associated with inferior survival in comparison to mutations (Fig. 7A). Unlike lung tumors with ERBB2 in-frame mutations, which typically function as “solitary drivers” in a relatively “quiet” genomic background, ERBB2-amplified cases were characterized by a heavier burden of co-occurring alterations, representing a biologically more aggressive entity. Together, these findings indicate that ERBB2 mutations and amplification represent biologically distinct subgroups with potentially different therapeutic vulnerabilities, reinforcing the need for alteration type and tissue-specific approaches to HER2-directed precision oncology.

The heterogeneity observed in ERBB2-amplified tumors highlights important clinical implications, particularly concerning patient prognosis. We identified distinct co-occurring alterations associated with a poor prognosis, most notably MAP2K1 mutations in COADREAD, CCNE1 amplification in IDC, MDM2 amplification in LUAD, and ARID1A mutation in STAD. Given that our study is a retrospective, descriptive clinicogenomic analysis lacking direct drug-response data, these findings cannot definitively establish causality or define precise mechanisms of therapeutic resistance. Instead, these observations must be interpreted as strictly hypothesis-generating, raising the possibility that concurrent activation of the MAPK pathway, cell-cycle deregulation, or chromatin remodeling might contribute to aggressive tumor biology. Rigorous functional validation and prospective clinical studies are therefore essential to confirm whether these co-alterations actively attenuate the efficacy of HER2-targeted therapies. Collectively, our findings underscore the translational potential of integrating co-mutational profiles into future research, providing a conceptual framework that may eventually guide biomarker-driven stratification in HER2-directed clinical trials.

Our study sheds light on the rare phenomenon of ERBB2 compound mutations. ERBB2 compound mutations were identified in less than 1% of cases across both cohorts. Although our exploratory pooled survival analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant prognostic difference between patients with simple and compound ERBB2 mutations, we caution against drawing definitive negative conclusions from this result. Given the limited number of events in the compound mutation subgroup, this observation must be considered strictly preliminary. Furthermore, these findings must be interpreted in the context that the vast majority of cases were treated before the introduction of covalent or potent reversible HER2-selective TKIs such as zongertinib and sevabertinib. As these novel agents become standard of care, the clinical landscape may shift. It is possible that differences in drug sensitivity between simple and compound mutants, which may not be apparent under current regimens, could translate into distinct prognostic outcomes in the context of these TKIs. Moreover, the deployment of such inhibitors will intensify selective pressure on the kinase domain, potentially leading to an increased frequency of compound mutations as a mechanism of acquired on-target resistance. Continued genomic surveillance and functional studies will therefore be essential to monitor shifts in the prevalence and clinical significance of ERBB2 compound mutations in the era of selective HER2-targeted therapy.

Several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. First, this study is limited by its retrospective design. Second, differences in sequencing platforms, panel composition, and copy-number calling algorithms across GENIE and C-CAT introduce potential variability in the detection of ERBB2 amplification and co-occurring alterations, although our panel-level comparisons suggest that these effects are most pronounced for amplification rather than mutations. Finally, our study is descriptive in nature, and functional validation was beyond its scope. Therefore, the mechanistic impact of individual ERBB2 hotspots or co-mutations, particularly in the setting of emerging HER2-targeted therapies, requires further investigation.

In conclusion, this large-scale, cross-population analysis elucidates the complex and highly heterogeneous landscape of ERBB2-altered cancers. We demonstrate that “ERBB2-altered cancer” is not a homogenous entity but rather a collection of distinct diseases defined by histological context, specific alteration types, and unique co-mutation profiles. As next-generation HER2-selective TKIs and ADCs continue to expand treatment options, our results offer a foundational framework for mutation-specific therapeutic stratification. Such stratification will guide the rational selection of emerging therapies and ultimately improve the clinical outcomes for patients with diverse ERBB2-driven malignancies.

Methods

Data sources and study population

This study analyzed genomic and clinical data from two large-scale, real-world cancer genomic databases: AACR Project GENIE (v18.0) in the USA and C-CAT in Japan36. The mutation and copy number variation data of AACR Project GENIE, TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas, and MSK-IMPACT were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) and Synapse (https://genie.synapse.org/). Anonymized tissue comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) data were obtained from the C-CAT portal site. As of November 2025, three tissue-based CGP tests have been approved in Japan: FoundationOne CDx, OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel, and GenMineTOP37. Of note, the C-CAT database collects clinicogenomic data from patients undergoing CGP under the Japanese national health insurance system, which limits the indication to advanced or recurrent solid tumors in patients who have completed or will soon complete standard therapies. Cases analyzed using liquid CGP were excluded because some patients may not harbor sufficient circulating tumor DNA, potentially leading to false-negative findings38. Cancer types were classified according to the OncoTree. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, the University of Tokyo (2021341G), and the Information Utilization Review Board of C-CAT (CDU2022-026N). Informed consent was obtained from the cancer patients before they were registered in the C-CAT database.

Definitions of gene alterations

Mutations were annotated based on ClinVar and OncoKB. Variant allele frequency cutoffs followed each platform’s reporting standards. Gene copy number alterations were defined according to the copy number calling algorithm used by each platform. TMB was defined using the values reported by each CGP platform. TMB-high was defined as ≥10 mutations/Mb.

Statistical analyses

Differences in categorical variables (co-mutation frequency, TMB-high, sex) were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. For multi-gene co-alteration analyses, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. Age differences were assessed using Student’s t-test. Overall survival was calculated from the date of sample collection to the date of death or last follow-up. Overall survival probability was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and unadjusted differences were assessed using the log-rank test. To identify independent prognostic factors and adjust for potential confounders, multivariate survival analyses were subsequently performed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. The multivariate models included age, sex, and the number of prior treatment lines (≥ 3 vs. < 3 lines) as covariates. Because survival comparisons were conducted across numerous clinical and genomic strata without adjustment for multiple hypothesis testing, these p-values should be interpreted as exploratory signals.

Data analyses

All analyses were performed in Python (version 3.9.5). Circos plots were generated using pycirclize (ver. 1.9.1), and Oncoprints were generated using pyoncoprint (ver. 0.2.5).
