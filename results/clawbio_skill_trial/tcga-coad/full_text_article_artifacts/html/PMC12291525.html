KRAS amplification in colorectal cancer: correlations with clinicopathological features and prognosis in patients and prediction of response to targeted therapy

Xuanhua Yang, Shiwei Feng, Qi Zhang, Wenxin Li, Shujuan Li, Min Zhi, Ping Lan, Sijing Cheng
Journal of Translational Medicine

Abstract

Background

Kirsten Rat Sarcoma (KRAS) copy number amplification has emerged as an oncogenic driver in colorectal cancer (CRC), in addition to KRAS mutation. However, its clinical significance remains poorly understood. Notably, CRC patients with wild-type KRAS but harboring KRAS amplification have shown resistance to anti-EGFR therapy, representing an unmet clinical need.

Methods

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the impact of KRAS amplification—alone and in conjunction with mutation—on clinicopathological characteristics, immune infiltration, and therapeutic response. KRAS copy number variation (CNV) was classified into amplification and non-amplification groups. KRAS mutational status was determined as wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) using qPCR and Sanger sequencing. CD8⁺ T lymphocyte infiltration was evaluated by immunohistochemistry in resected CRC specimens. Clinical, immune, and survival data were analyzed in association with KRAS status. RNA-seq was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enriched pathways. Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models were used to assess responses to targeted therapies.

Results

We found that KRAS amplification was more frequent in WT KRAS CRC (21.4%) than in MUT KRAS CRC (6.5%). In the WT subgroup, KRAS amplification was associated with poor prognosis and increased KRAS protein expression and downstream pathway activation. In contrast, amplification had little effect in mutant KRAS tumors. KRAS copy number was inversely correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, suggesting a role in immune evasion. Importantly, low CD8⁺ T cell density combined with KRAS amplification predicted adverse outcomes. Therapeutically, KRAS-amplified PDX models were resistant to anti-EGFR treatment. However, combined MEK and CDK4/6 inhibition overcame this resistance.

Conclusions

KRAS amplification constitutes a distinct oncogenic driver in CRC and a potential biomarker for therapeutic stratification. Our findings support the clinical rationale for dual MEK and CDK4/6 inhibition in KRAS-amplified CRC and highlight the need for biomarker-guided clinical trials to optimize treatment strategies in this subset of patients.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second-leading cause of cancer death worldwide and has a high incidence rate [1]. While traditional treatments, such as surgery and chemotherapy, are the main treatments for CRC, they have a poor prognosis. Therefore, molecular targets need to be identified providing further therapeutic strategy [2]. The Kirsten Rat Sarcoma (KRAS) is one of the commonest mutated oncogenes in CRC, and its activation are believed to be a vital driver of CRC initiation and progression, as evidenced by the nearly ubiquitous alterations of oncogenic RAS pathway. EGFR-targeted therapy is an effective means to treat wild-type RAS in CRC patients. However, it is reported that 35–45% of patients still exhibit resistance and don't respond to anti-EGFR therapy in the wild-type RAS CRC [3], indicating the existence of other molecular mechanisms besides mutation that may drive resistance to anti-EGFR therapy. Previous studies have suggested that KRAS copy number variant (CNV) may be one such mechanism, namely KRAS amplification may represent an oncogenic driver and mediator of drug resistance in malignancies [4].

Genetic variation makes one unique in terms of disease susceptibility. CNV is now recognized as one of the most important forms of genetic variation [5]. CNV is stable and heritable generally, any processes of CNV may cause disruption of genes, which may lead to diseases [6]. Pulciani was the first to find that transduction of a cell line with amplification of wild RAS oncogene was sufficient to induce malignant transformation in the absence of activating mutations [7]. In recent years, the contribution of CNV to the susceptibility of diseases has been the focal point of research. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that the tumors bearing CNV are associated with advanced disease status, poor tumor differentiation, and inferior survival in patients [8–12]. As an acknowledged target in the therapy of tumor, KRAS amplification were found to be associated with poor survival rate, which could be an independent prognostic variable [13]. However, due to mutation, the extent to which KRAS amplification contributes to CRC is not well understood. Besides, the effect of the interaction of these two above variants on prognosis and prediction has not been well elucidated.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) involves interactions between immune and tumor cells, and plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and patient prognosis [14–16]. Recently, the relationship between CNV and immune infiltration in tumors has received an increasing number of attention because this feature can be used to improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. An analysis based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) on 5255 tumors representing 12 cancer types revealed a negative relationship between CNV and immune cell infiltration [17]. KRAS mutation is thought to be a key factor contributing to the universal immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in CRC. Previous our studies have established that KRAS regulate CRC immune infiltrates via specific signaling pathways, and CD8+ T cells could be the main targets [18]. However, the functional role of KRAS amplification in CRC immune microenvironment has hardly been reported.

Although therapeutic strategies for KRAS-mutant CRC have been widely studied, there is a growing interest in understanding the clinical implications of KRAS amplification, which represents a distinct and understudied molecular subtype. Recent evidence suggests that KRAS amplification is associated with resistance to anti-EGFR therapy in CRC, even in the absence of KRAS mutations, highlighting its potential role as an alternative resistance mechanism [19].Preclinical studies have demonstrated that dual targeting of the RAS/MAPK pathway—such as combining MEK and SHP2 inhibition—can overcome KRAS amplification–driven signaling [20]. SHP2 and SOS1 inhibitors (e.g., RMC-4630) are being evaluated in early-phase clinical trials and have shown tolerability and modest disease control in RAS-driven tumors [21]. Additionally, pan-RAS inhibitors such as RMC-6236, which can target wild-type KRAS, have demonstrated early signs of clinical activity in Phase I studies [22]. While clinical trials specifically enrolling patients with KRAS-amplified CRC remain limited, these emerging therapeutic strategies underscore the need for further mechanistic and translational investigation in this subset.

In regard of the problems above, we aim to conduct a comprehensive research of CRC harboring KRAS CNV for further analyzing the tumorigenic role of KRAS CNV, the optimal pharmacological approach for KRAS-pathway inhibition and the identification of KRAS CNV as a predictive biomarker in order to guide future diagnosis and therapy.

Methods

Patients and tissue samples

A total of 304 CRC tissue specimens, including 44 KRAS copy number amplification cases and 260 copy number non-amplification cases, were obtained from patients who had received radical surgery at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. (Guangzhou, China). All patients did not undergo either chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery. The exclusion criteria ruled out patients who had local excisions performed and patients with multiple primary CRC. Among these 304 CRC patients, there are 38 patients (12.50%) with Stage I, 148 patients (48.68%) with Stage II, 99 patients (32.57%) with Stage III and 19 patients (6.25%) with Stage IV. Clinical parameters were obtained from the patient's electronic medical records located in hospital. All human tissue samples were obtained with informed consent from donors, and all procedures were conducted with the permission of the Institutional Review Board of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University.

Determination of copy number and mutation

KRAS copy number were evaluated by real-time quantitative PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted by using a QIAamp DNA Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Santa Clarita, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA input was 100 ng, and quantitative real-time PCR reactions were performed at 95 ℃ for 30 s followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. The primer sequences used were KRAS-F, 5'-GGCAAGAGTGCCTTGACGAT-3' and KRAS-R, 5'-CTCCTCTTGACCTGCT GTGT-3'; β-actin-F, 5'-TTGTTACAGGAAGTCCCTTGCC-3' and β-actin-R, 5'-ATGCTATCACCTCCCCTGTGTG-3', respectively. Real-time quantitative PCR using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed to check the DNA copy number. The copy number calculation was carried out using the comparative Ct method [23]. The copy number greater than 3 is considered as amplification; otherwise, it is non-amplification, as in a previous study [24]. KRAS mutation status was assessed by Sanger sequencing.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted by tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration of protein samples was analyzed with the BCA standard protein kit, and quantitative experiments were carried out on the basis of its instructions. 5 × SDS loading buffer was used. 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane was used to transfer protein from the 8% running gel (Servicebio) and the blots were blocked with 5% nonfat milk. Then incubated with specific primary antibodies against KRAS (Abcam,1:1000), ERK (CST,1:1000), p-ERK (CST,1:1000), MEK (CST,1:1000), p-MEK (CST,1:1000), AKT (CST,1:1000), p-AKT (CST,1:1000) and β-Actin (Proteintech,1:2000). Membranes were incubated with a specific secondary antibody included anti-mouse (Proteintech,1:10,000) and anti-rabbit (Proteintech,1:10,000), and visualized by ECL reagents.

Immunohistochemistry and IHC scoring

Paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized with xylene, followed by antigen retrieval with EDTA pH 8.0 buffer. The normal goat serum was used to block tissues at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, the anti-CD8 antibody (Abcam,1:2000) as primary antibodies were used to incubate tissues at 4 °C overnight. Then appropriate secondary antibodies and DAB peroxidase substrate were used to incubate the slides. Finally, the slides were counterstained with haematoxylin. Positive CD8 T cells densities were calculated by counting the number of positive cells per tissue sample using tumor tissue sections [25]. CD8⁺ T cell density was dichotomized into high and low groups using the median value as the cut-off, a commonly applied method in IHC-based analyses when no established threshold exists.

RNA-Seq and data analysis

In order to explore the transcriptomic landscape changes associated with CRC by copy number, four CRC tissues of wild KRAS amplification and four CRC tissues of wild KRAS non-amplification were collected for RNA sequencing. Tumor samples were divided into two groups based on copy number status. Using TRizol reagent (Invitrogen), total RNA was isolated from colorectal cancer tumor tissues. Ribosomal RNA was removed using the RiboMinus Eukaryote Kit (Qiagen), and a cDNA library was created by a NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs). cDNA fragments were purified, and end repaired, and a poly(A) tail was added. Using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, deep sequencing was carried out, aligning the clean reads to the reference genome. DESeq2 software was used to examine the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) comparing KRAS amplification with non-amplification tumor tissues in wild type. The genes with the parameter of absolute fold change(|log2FC|) > 2 and FDR < 0.01 were defined differentially expressed genes. To study the biological roles of DEGs, GeneOntology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted for biological process (BP), cellularcomponent (CC) and molecular function (MF). In addition, DEGs were subjected to KyotoEncyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis.

Patient derived xenograft models

All animal care and experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sun Yat-sen University. For the construction of CRC PDX model, fresh tumor tissues were obtained from CRC patients and implanted into NCG mice. When the tumor size reached 1.5 cm, the tumors were divided into equal volume ~ 2 mm and were subcutaneously implanted into 4–5 weeks male NOD/SCID mice [26]. All mice were randomly divided into several groups (n = 5 per group): treated with injection of appropriate targeted drug: EGFR inhibitor (Cetuximab was administered intraperitoneally at 1 mg/kg, twice weekly), MEK inhibitor (Trametinib was given via oral gavage at 3 mg/kg every 2 days), CDK4/6 inhibitor (Palbociclib or Ribociclib was administered at 75 mg/kg daily via oral gavage).Tumor sizes and body weight were recorded every week after treatment, and tumor volumes were calculated according to the formula: tumor volume (mm3) = length × (width)2 × 0.5. All mice were sacrificed 3 weeks later. Excessive tumor size (more than 2 cm in diameter), or body weight loss greater than 20% were taken into consideration for ethical endpoints in this study. All mice were fed in pathogen-free conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software or SPSS 25.0 (SPSS 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. For continuous variables with normal distributions, a two‐tailed Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA was employed to establish statistical significance, whereas either a Mann–Whitney U‐test or a Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for skewed distributions. For survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier plots and log‐rank tests were employed. All P‐values were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined as a P‐value of 0.05.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis of KRAS-amplified CRC

Both gene mutation and copy number variation belong to genomic variation. Considering KRAS played important role in CRC, we performed a comprehensive analysis to explore the relationship between genomic variation of KRAS and clinical characteristics of CRC. By analyzing the distributional difference of KRAS amplification in different mutation status, we found that KRAS amplification group mainly occurred in KRAS wild type (KRASWT) (21.4%, 28/131); In contrast, only 6 patients with had amplification in KRAS mutant type (KRASMUT) (6.5%, 6/92) (Fig. 1E). Further, we divided the samples into four groups according to KRAS copy number variation and mutation: wild-type KRAS non-amplification group, wild-type KRAS amplification group, mutant KRAS non-amplification group, and mutant KRAS non-amplification group. It was found that there were significant differences in tumor stage, immune score and tumor location among the four groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1F). In KRASWT, the expression of KRAS protein in the copy number amplification group was significantly higher than that in the non-amplification group. However, no matter in which type of KRAS mutation, there was no significant difference in KRAS expression between the amplification and non-amplification groups (Fig. 1G). We next analyzed DFS and OS of CRC patients with KRAS CNV in different mutation status. CNV had no significant effect on the OS and DFS of patients in KRASMUT (60 months, P > 0.05), and prognosis of KRASWT CRC patients with amplification were significantly inferior than those without amplification (60 months, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1H). We further validated the clinical prognostic significance of KRAS wild-type amplification using the TCGA dataset via the cBioPortal platform [27]. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with KRAS gene amplification exhibited significantly poorer OS (log-rank test P = 0.0117) and DFS (log-rank test P = 0.0106) compared to those without amplification (Supplementary Fig. S1). These independent findings are consistent with our primary results and further support KRAS amplification as a potential adverse prognostic marker in colorectal cancer. Overall, KRAS CNV was closely associated with prognosis.

KRAS amplification is correlated with low CD8+ T cells infiltration

Due to the important role of KRAS in tumor immunity, we hypothesized that KRAS copy number variants are associated with tumor immune escape. To test this hypothesis, we quantified the density of CD8+ T cells in 204 CRC tissue samples using immunohistochemistry to assess the association between KRAS CNV and tumor infiltration. The result showed there was no significant difference in the CD8+ T cells density between the amplification group and the non-amplification group in KRASMUT (n = 87) (P > 0.05). However, in KRASWT (n = 117), the density of CD8 + T cells in amplification group was significantly lower than in KRAS non-amplification group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). To further validate the association between KRAS amplification and reduced T cell infiltration, we analyzed publicly available human CRC single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets from the TISCH (Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub) platform [28], which revealed a mutually exclusive spatial distribution between KRAS-high tumor regions and CD8⁺ T cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2). KRAS variation results in abnormal activation of the corresponding phosphorylation signaling pathway downstream. However, due to the high heterogeneity of KRAS tumors, the phosphorylation signaling pathways and their mutual compensatory and feedback regulation are great differences. Given the centrality of MAPK signaling to KRAS-driven cancer, we firstly explore the efficacy of MEK inhibitor in KRAS-amplified CRC and found MEK inhibitor monotherapy has produced suboptimal results in animal models due to the development of resistance. Therefore, we sought to gain insight into the efficacy of targeted combination therapy in CRC with amplification of KRAS. We constructed a KRAS-amplified PDX model mice with a combination of MEKi with CD4/6i. The results indicated that compared to MEKi treatment alone, a combination therapy of CD4/6i significantly enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy as shown by slower tumor growth (Fig. 5H, I). Therefore, combination of MEKi and CDK4/6i may be a promising treatment strategy against KRAS amplification in CRC.

Discussion

KRAS is recognized as the most common oncogene in human cancers. The role of CNV in CRC cannot be ignored. Therefore, this study mainly focuses on the clinicopathological characteristics of KRAS CNV in different mutation status and the role in immune infiltration, as well as targeted therapy.

Given the higher morbidity of CNV in tumors, we examined the KRAS amplification in CRC, and found that KRAS amplification was more common in wild-type KRAS. The proportion of patients with both KRAS mutation and copy number gain was rather small, indicating a mutually exclusive relationship between KRAS mutation and amplification was identified. Modrek and colleagues also found similar results. Among 166 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), only 6.6% had KRAS mutation with amplification [29]. Similarly, in the surgical tissue samples of 172 NSCLC patients evaluated by Sasaki, only 8 (4.6%) had KRAS mutations and gene copy number gains. As a tumor driver, genetic mutation is sufficient to initiate a tumor and maintain its malignant proliferation. Gene mutation occurs randomly in individual cells, and those that confer a selective advantage may preclude clonal expansion from being fixed in tumor cell populations, so copy-number amplification is less common in the presence of activating mutations. However, this trend needs to be further verified in a larger cohort sample.

Our data also further confirmed that KRAS amplification is significantly associated with poor prognosis in CRC. According to univariate Cox analysis, KRAS amplification had a significant impact on the overall prognosis of CRC patients, especially in advanced CRC patients. Of note, copy number amplification retained prognostic value in multivariate COX regression analysis after adjustment for confounding factors. This suggests that KRAS amplification is important in the multi-factorial, multi-step development of CRC and that it may be a clinically useful independent prognostic factor. KRAS amplification is a clinically actionable alteration in CRC that can be readily assessed through existing NGS panels without added cost or workflow disruption. It is associated with poor prognosis and may serve as a complementary biomarker for stratifying KRAS wild-type patients and guiding therapeutic decisions. Its presence may predict resistance to anti-EGFR therapy and inform the use of combination strategies such as MEK and CDK4/6 inhibition. These findings support incorporating KRAS amplification into routine molecular profiling to enhance personalized treatment in CRC. In conclusion, KRAS amplification can be used as a potential adverse prognostic marker for CRC patients and play an important role in predicting and guiding the clinical treatment of CRC patients.

Wild-type KRAS amplification may be another way to cause aberrant KRAS proteins to be "locked" in their activated state. The mechanism of KRAS canonical mutational activation has been well established in tumors. Activating mutation in the KRAS leads to an increased KRAS-GTP activity, ultimately resulting in the tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis inhibition via the KRAS downstream pathway [30–32]. It is worth mentioning that KRAS activation status is not only achieved by mutant pattern. In addition to mutation, KRAS CNV may also be involved in the regulatory mechanism of KRAS-GTP activity [20, 33]. Pulciani found that cell lines with wild-type RAS proto-oncogenes with increased copy number were also sufficient to induce malignant transformation in the absence of activating mutations [7]. Mita used MKN1 gastric cancer cells to verify that overexpression of endogenous wild-type KRAS is likely to induce biological effects similar to KRAS mutations [34]. Expanded wild-type KRAS may provide a growth advantage to cancer cells. Here, we further evaluated the effects of KRAS amplification on the classical pathway under the influence of KRAS mutation. Specifically, an excellent concordance among amplification and KRAS protein expression and downstream activation in wild-type KRAS. In contrast, since KRAS signaling is already in an activated state in mutant KRAS, CNV has little effect on the expression of KRAS and downstream. These might partly explain the poor prognosis observed in patients with wild-type KRAS with amplification. Recent studies indicate that wild-type KRAS amplification activates downstream signaling by increasing KRAS protein levels and Ras-GTP activity, leading to sustained MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathway activation. Mita et al. showed that knockdown of amplified KRAS in gastric cancer cells reduced ERK and AKT phosphorylation, confirming a dosage-dependent effect [34]. Wong et al. demonstrated that KRAS-amplified tumors maintain ERK signaling via rapid feedback through RTKs, SHP2, and SOS1/2, even under MEK inhibition [20]. Bahcall et al. reported that amplified KRAS drives autocrine EGFR ligand secretion to sustain signaling independently of upstream receptor inhibition [35]. Li et al. further identified SHP2 as a central node mediating MAPK signaling in KRAS-amplified gastroesophageal tumors [33]. It is acknowledged that Tumor infiltrating immune cells are prognostic factors of CRC, and the infiltration is also correlated with KRAS activated status [37–39]. Considering that KRAS amplification is also one of the ways to activate this oncogene, we analyze the role of KRAS amplification in tumor immune response in the CRC cohort and found KRAS amplification was associated with low infiltration fraction of CD8+ T cells in CRC patients. On this basis, we further found this correlation only appeared in KRAS wild type, and did not show no significant difference in KRAS mutant type. We consider this is due to the fact that CNV is the major mechanism for activating KRAS "molecular switch" and downstream pathways in the KRAS wild-type rather than KRAS mutant-type. In addition, Davoli use The Cancer Genome Atlas project and found that immune evasion was related to CNV at the chromosomal level and was mainly affected by gene dosage rather than the effect of specific genes [17]. This is also consistent with our finding that KRAS amplified protein expression was significantly greater than that of the KRAS non-amplified protein in the wild type, and matched by the density of CD8+ T cells. Studies have shown that high density of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment has been confirmed to be associated with a good prognosis. Our subgroup study also found that KRAS CNV might also affect the prognosis by regulating the CD8+ T cells infiltration level, which indicated that KRAS CNV might be a target to regulate the immune response of CRC. The increased abundance of CD8+ T cells as first-line defense cells against tumor progression might partly compensate for the poor prognosis caused by KRAS amplification. KRAS amplification may promote immune evasion by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. It upregulates PD-L1 through RAS–MEK, ROS–p38, and PI3K pathways, suppressing CD8⁺ T cell activity [40], and induces IL-10, TGF-β, GM-CSF, and IL-8, which recruit MDSCs and polarize M2 macrophages [41, 42]. Sustained KRAS signaling may also downregulate MHC I expression, impairing antigen presentation. These changes contribute to a “cold” immune phenotype with low CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and increased immunosuppressive cell populations.

In clinical trials to date, anti-EGFR therapy have produced a large clinical benefits in wild-type KRAS of CRC. However, there are still a small number of patients, especially those with wild KRAS amplification is insensitive to EGFR blockade due to their capacity to adaptively respond by rapidly increasing KRAS-GTP levels [20]. Therefore, KRAS amplification also has been regarded as a mechanism of resistance to anti-EGFR therapy. Although several combination therapies, has increased the objective response rate for patients with KRAS-mutant CRC in recent years [22], there is currently no approved targeted therapy for KRAS amplification in CRC. In view of lacking standard treatment options, there is an urgent need to explore new therapeutic strategies for KRAS-amplified CRC to improve patient prognosis. Given the centrality of MAPK signaling to KRAS-driven tumor, specifically the mutant KRAS tumor, the targeted therapy around MAPK pathway has been concerned. However, mutant KRAS has multiple regulatory mechanisms and inhibition of the downstream pathway could be affected by reactivation of the parallel pathway, which in turn creates further resistance [22]. Previous studies have shown poor efficacy of MAPK signaling target inhibitor monotherapy in mutant KRAS [43–45]. Our results show some parallels to efforts to establish therapy for mutant KRAS CRC, which is also less sensitive to MEK inhibitor monotherapy. Previous studies have shown that CDK4/6i would inhibit phosphorylation of S6, which is associated with tumor growth factor signaling through the MEK/ERK and PI3K/mTOR pathways. Therefore, CD4/6i was found to significantly sensitized mutant KRAS mice to MEKi [46, 47]. Mechanistically, the enhanced efficacy of MEK and CDK4/6 co-inhibition in KRAS-amplified tumors arises from dual targeting of interconnected oncogenic processes. KRAS amplification drives sustained MAPK activation and upregulates cell cycle regulators such as cyclin D1, leading to dependency on CDK4/6-mediated G1–S progression [48]. While MEK inhibition is often limited by compensatory feedback via RTKs or the PI3K-AKT pathway, CDK4/6 inhibition bypasses these mechanisms and directly suppresses cell cycle progression [33]. Compared to other combinations, such as MEKi plus PI3K inhibitors, the MEKi + CDK4/6i regimen offers more coherent targeting of KRAS-driven signaling and proliferation [49]. Preclinical models confirm that this strategy induces durable inhibition of tumor growth in KRAS-amplified CRC, supporting its translational potential (Fig. 5H–I). Along these lines, we explored combined therapy for KRAS amplification and found the KRAS-amplified CRC patients also emerge as potential candidates for dual targeting of CDK4/6 and MEK.

Together, these results uncover the therapeutic potential for targeting of KRAS-driven tumors through combination therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the lack of an external validation cohort prevents us from confirming the reproducibility of the results in independent patient populations. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and prospective designs are needed to validate our observations and further explore the clinical utility of KRAS amplification as a biomarker.

Conclusion

In summary, KRAS amplification represents an alternative mechanism of KRAS activation in the absence of KRAS mutations, contributing to the activation of downstream oncogenic signaling pathways in CRC. Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between KRAS amplification and mutation status in Chinese CRC patients. We further characterize the clinicopathological features and immune landscape associated with KRAS amplification across different KRAS mutation subtypes. Importantly, our preclinical findings demonstrate that combined MEK and CDK4/6 inhibition effectively overcomes resistance to anti-EGFR therapy in KRAS-amplified CRC models. These results provide a strong rationale for the development of biomarker-driven trials incorporating KRAS amplification status to optimize therapeutic decision-making in CRC.
